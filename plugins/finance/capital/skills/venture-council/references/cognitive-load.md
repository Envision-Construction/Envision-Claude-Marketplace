# Cognitive Load & Information Structure — verified doctrine

Why the bandwidth law exists. Every claim below survived an adversarial fact-check
(2026-07); the blacklist at the bottom is what did NOT survive — never cite it.

## The science (what a partner's brain actually does)

- **Working memory holds 3–5 chunks (~4) of novel material** — Cowan (2001, *BBS*;
  2010, "The Magical Mystery Four"). Miller's 1956 "7±2" was inflated by rehearsal and
  chunking; nobody defends 7 for unchunked input. Chunk size barely matters — capacity
  is fixed in chunks (Mathy & Feldman 2012: ~3–4 compressed items). **Rule: a pitch is
  ~3 points; a slide is ≤4 new facts.**
- **Cognitive Load Theory** — Sweller (1988; 2010): total load = intrinsic (the idea's
  real complexity) + extraneous (imposed by presentation) + germane (schema-building).
  Jargon, acronyms, and nested clauses are pure extraneous load: they burn
  working-memory slots on decoding instead of on the argument.
- **Processing fluency = credibility** — Alter & Oppenheimer (2009, *PSPR*): easy-to-
  process statements are judged more TRUE, more likable, more familiar. Oppenheimer
  (2006): needlessly complex vocabulary makes the *author* be judged less intelligent.
  Plain language isn't dumbing down; it's how claims get believed.
- **Serial-position effect** — Murdock (1962); Glanzer & Cunitz (1966): recall is
  U-shaped — first items (primacy) and last items (recency) survive; the middle dies.
  Recency fades after a filled delay, so **primacy is what reaches Monday partner
  meeting.** Core claim in the first breath; ask restated at the close.
- **Narrative is a chunk** — Green & Brock (2000, *JPSP*, replicated in later
  meta-analyses): transportation into a story increases persuasion and suppresses
  counterarguing. Problem→insight→proof told as one arc costs one chunk; the same
  content as six facts costs six.
- **Hick's law + choice overload** — decision time grows with log of options (Hick
  1952/Hyman 1953). Iyengar & Lepper (2000): 24 jams → 3% bought; 6 jams → 30%.
  Chernev et al. (2015) meta-analysis (99 observations, N=7,202): overload is
  conditional — strongest under time pressure, complexity, and preference uncertainty,
  i.e., exactly a partner meeting. **One ask.**
- **System 1 / System 2** — Kahneman (2011): partners skim in fast automatic System 1;
  anything demanding computation (undefined acronyms, mental math, nested logic) forces
  reluctant System 2 engagement that is *felt* as friction and skepticism. Pre-compute
  every number.
- **Concreteness** — concrete, imageable statements are remembered better than
  abstractions (picture-superiority literature; mechanism debated, effect robust).
  "The robot ties rebar all night" beats "autonomous jobsite task execution."

## The structure stack (MECE, modernized)

MECE — Barbara Minto's grouping test from McKinsey (no overlaps, no gaps) — is
necessary hygiene but guarantees nothing about *prioritization, ordering, or
memorability*, which is where pitches die. The modern stack layers on top:

| Layer | Rule a reviewer enforces | What it fixes over MECE alone |
|---|---|---|
| **Minto pyramid / SCQA** | Answer/recommendation FIRST, then grouped support (~3 per group, 2–4 convention — Minto never wrote a hard cap) | Which bucket leads; reader can stop at any depth and still hold the conclusion |
| **BLUF** (AR 25-50) | Sentence one of any email/memo states the ask — could the recipient act on sentence one alone? | Enforces answer-first at the message level, in the first 5 seconds |
| **Progressive disclosure** (Nielsen, NN/g 2006) | One-liner → teaser deck → memo → data room; each layer self-sufficient; >2 forced levels loses people | Rations detail to the audience's current attention level — the actual scarce resource |
| **One idea per slide** (Duarte: "if that means 50 slides for a 15-minute presentation, so be it") | If a slide's takeaway isn't one sentence, split it | Attention-unit granularity — a MECE deck can still cram 3 clean ideas onto one slide and lose all three |
| **Assertion-evidence** (Garner & Alley 2013, *IJEE* 29(6)) | Slide titles are falsifiable sentences ("82% of pilots convert"), never noun phrases ("Traction") | Comprehension + testability: buckets vs findings |
| **Kawasaki 10/20/30** | 10 slides, 20 minutes, no font under 30pt — three mechanical checks | Hard scope caps; MECE can justify unbounded exhaustiveness |
| **Narrative memo** (Bezos, 2017 shareholder letter; six-pagers) | If the argument can't survive as connected prose, the thinking isn't done | Bullets — even MECE ones — let broken logic hide between the lines |
| **One number per slide** (practitioner convention, no single origin) | Each data slide has exactly one metric at maximum visual weight | The retellable statistic |

**Attention reality check (DocSend telemetry):** average investor time-on-deck ~2:30
(2021), under 2 minutes at seed by 2023 — and more reading time does NOT predict
success (2020 pre-seed data: failed decks got *more* time than winning ones, 3:30 vs
3:21 — confusion reads slow). The deck must land its full argument in a sub-3-minute
skim.

## Folklore blacklist — never cite these

- **"Attention span shorter than a goldfish / 8 seconds"** — untraceable statistic
  laundered through a 2015 Microsoft Canada report; debunked (BBC 2017).
- **Mehrabian's "93% of communication is nonverbal" (7-38-55)** — applies only to
  feelings/attitudes under channel conflict, from two 1967 single-word studies;
  Mehrabian himself disowns the generalization.
- **"System 1 makes 96% of decisions"** — not in Kahneman; invented in summaries.
- **"People remember 65% of pictures after 3 days"** — *Brain Rules* figure with no
  solid primary source.
- **The learning pyramid ("we remember 10% of what we read…")** — fabricated
  percentages (Letrud & Hernes 2018).
- **Learning styles (visual/auditory learners)** — no supporting evidence (Pashler et
  al. 2008, *PSPI*).
- **"We use 10% of our brain"** — false.
- **"Minto caps groups at exactly 3"** — convention is 2–4; no hard cap in her text.
