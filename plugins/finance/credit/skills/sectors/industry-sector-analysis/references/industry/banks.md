# Banks Credit Analysis

## Sector Overview

Banks span money-center, super-regional, regional, community, custody, trust, and specialized card-issuing institutions. Credit quality is driven less by debt/EBITDA than by funding durability, asset quality, capital, liquidity, and confidence sensitivity. The core underwriting task is to determine whether earnings capacity, reserves, and capital can absorb a normal recession without forcing dilutive capital raises, regulatory intervention, or destabilizing funding outflows.

Market structure differs by business model. Global and money-center banks benefit from scale, diversified fee streams, and systemic importance, but they also carry greater market, legal, and operational complexity. Regional and community banks rely more heavily on spread income and local deposit franchises, making them more sensitive to commercial real estate concentration, securities marks, and deposit competition. Custody and trust banks have lower credit risk in their loan books but higher sensitivity to fee rates, rates, and operational resilience.

### Calibration lens: capital, funding, and earnings absorption

**Why banks cannot be judged like generic corporates**

- **Deposits are a liability but also the franchise.** Cheap, granular, sticky deposits support earnings and resilience; concentrated or rate-sensitive deposits can reverse quickly under stress.
- **Loan growth consumes capital.** A bank can report strong revenue growth while actually weakening credit if it is stretching underwriting, lengthening duration, or adding concentrated exposures faster than capital formation.
- **Reported earnings can flatter true resilience.** Provision releases, unrealized securities marks outside regulatory capital, or unusually favorable deposit betas can make a point-in-time ROE look stronger than through-cycle earning power.

Always anchor bank analysis to capital, liquidity, and funding stability first; profitability and growth are secondary if they are not supported by defensible underwriting and a stable liability base.

## Dimension 1: Cash Flow Quality and Predictability

### 1.1 Revenue / Income Visibility

Bank revenue ranges from recurring spread income to fee streams tied to servicing, trust, treasury management, cards, custody, and advisory. Revenue quality depends on how repeatable those sources remain after a shift in rates, deposit pricing, or credit costs.

- **Net interest margin (NIM)**: The core spread between earning-asset yields and funding costs. Healthy NIM is only useful if it is supported by durable deposit pricing, not a temporary lag in liability repricing.
- **Deposit mix and beta**: Non-interest-bearing and operating deposits are the highest-quality funding. Rising deposit beta, brokered deposits, and promotional CDs usually signal weaker franchise strength.
- **Fee income composition**: Treasury management, custody, servicing, and interchange are more recurring than mortgage banking gains, syndication fees, or volatile capital-markets revenue.
- **Loan growth quality**: Growth should be evaluated by segment, pricing, underwriting, and reserve build, not only by volume.
- **Securities income and balance-sheet carry**: Banks can appear stable when securities books are still earning legacy yields, but reinvestment at lower spreads or forced sales can change the earnings profile quickly.

*Analytical questions:*

- *Is revenue stability coming from a genuine franchise or from favorable short-term rate and funding conditions?*
- *How much of current earnings would remain if deposit costs normalized and provisioning reverted to through-cycle levels?*

### 1.2 Cyclicality and Macro Sensitivity

Banks are moderately to highly cyclical because credit costs and funding behavior change sharply across the cycle.

- **Credit cycle**: Provisioning and loss recognition are the most important cyclical swing factor.
- **Rate sensitivity**: Asset-sensitive banks initially benefit from higher rates, but prolonged rate pressure can compress loan demand and raise deposit costs.
- **Yield curve shape**: Inversions often pressure NIM and discourage maturity transformation.
- **Commercial real estate and business formation**: Regional banks are especially sensitive to local property, SME, and construction cycles.
- **Consumer stress transmission**: Card, indirect auto, and unsecured consumer portfolios tend to weaken before prime commercial books.

### 1.3 Seasonality and Cash Flow Timing

- Deposit balances often move around tax dates, year-end treasury cycles, and municipal/public fund calendars.
- Mortgage and capital-markets fees can be materially seasonal.
- Provisioning can appear lumpy because management reserves ahead of visible charge-offs.
- Securities repositioning, swap hedges, and AOCI movements can change reported capital and earnings timing without changing franchise quality.

### 1.4 Concentration Risk

- **Deposit concentration**: Large uninsured, venture, sponsor, municipal, or sector-concentrated deposits create run risk.
- **Loan concentration**: Office CRE, construction, sponsor finance, agriculture, or healthcare specialization can produce correlated downside.
- **Geographic concentration**: Single-state and single-MSA lenders face localized recession and property risk.
- **Funding-source concentration**: Heavy reliance on FHLB, brokered deposits, or wholesale borrowings reduces flexibility in stress.
- **Counterparty concentration**: For custody or institutional banks, a small set of large clients can matter more than gross account count suggests.

## Dimension 2: Margin and Cost Structure Resilience

### 2.1 Pricing Power and Rate-Setting Ability

- Loan pricing power depends on franchise strength, borrower competition, and product specialization.
- Deposit pricing power is strongest where the bank holds operating accounts, treasury relationships, and local branch relevance.
- Treasury, payments, and custody services often show better pricing resilience than commoditized lending.
- In competitive late-cycle environments, headline loan growth may indicate underpricing rather than strength.

### 2.2 Operating Leverage and Cost Flexibility

- Banks have mixed cost structures: technology, compliance, branches, and core operations are sticky; incentive compensation and marketing are more flexible.
- Efficiency ratio is useful, but aggressive cost cuts can weaken controls, sales execution, or deposit retention.
- Branch consolidation can support margins, but fast branch reduction may undermine local deposit durability.
- Scale matters in compliance, technology, fraud controls, and data investment.

### 2.3 Input Cost and Inflation Exposure

- **Funding costs** are the dominant input cost. Deposit repricing and wholesale spreads matter more than labor inflation in most cycles.
- **Technology and cybersecurity** spending is structurally rising and difficult to defer safely.
- **Compliance costs** increase with asset size thresholds, stress testing, AML obligations, and resolution planning.
- **Credit costs** are not an operating expense in the usual sense, but in practice they are the largest economic input to through-cycle returns.

### 2.4 Through-Cycle Margin Stability

- Stable banks preserve pre-provision net revenue even when NIM compresses.
- Fee-diversified institutions usually show more resilient through-cycle earnings than pure spread lenders.
- Custody and payments-heavy banks are less exposed to charge-offs but more exposed to fee compression and market-level swings.
- Banks that rely on exceptionally low deposit costs or reserve releases often look more stable than they truly are.

## Dimension 3: Capital Requirements and Asset Profile

### 3.1 Capital Expenditure / Investment Intensity

Traditional capex is modest, but banks are capital intensive because every asset added to the balance sheet consumes regulatory capital and liquidity resources.

- Technology modernization, digital channels, fraud controls, and risk infrastructure require sustained investment.
- Growth in risk-weighted assets can be more constraining than physical capex.
- Acquisition strategies must be judged by capital impact, integration complexity, and regulatory tolerance.

### 3.2 Working Capital and Liquidity Demands

- Liquidity is existential. Banks must survive both earnings stress and funding stress.
- Evaluate cash, secured borrowing capacity, HQLA, contingent liquidity, and deposit runoff assumptions.
- A bank with acceptable capital can still fail if deposit flight outruns available liquidity.
- Loan commitments, unfunded revolvers, and warehouse exposures can consume liquidity at the wrong moment.

### 3.3 Asset Quality, Tangibility, and Liquidation Value

- Loan-book composition, underwriting history, and vintage performance are the key asset-quality questions.
- Early-stage delinquencies, criticized/classified loans, non-accruals, and charge-offs are more informative than total reserve ratios alone.
- Securities portfolios require scrutiny for duration, unrealized losses, and liquidity value under stress.
- Tangible common equity and tangible book value remain core anchors for downside absorption.

### 3.4 Useful Life, Obsolescence, and Stranded Asset Risk

- Branch-heavy franchises face gradual obsolescence if digital channels outcompete legacy distribution.
- Narrow business models can be stranded by regulation, fintech competition, or loss of local relevance.
- Long-duration fixed-rate assets funded by unstable deposits create a form of balance-sheet obsolescence when rates reset.
- Legacy core systems can become a strategic weakness if they limit risk control, deposit retention, or product delivery.

## Dimension 4: Structural Protection and Leverage Capacity

### 4.1 Leverage and Coverage Calibration

Banks should be calibrated primarily through regulatory capital, tangible common equity, reserves, and earnings absorption, not corporate leverage ratios.

- **CET1, Tier 1, and total capital ratios** define the regulatory floor and buffer.
- **Tangible common equity / tangible assets** helps compare banks with different accounting and capital-stack complexity.
- **Pre-provision net revenue (PPNR)** is the key earnings-absorption metric in stress.
- **Allowance coverage** should be evaluated against loan mix, delinquency trends, and adverse scenarios rather than a generic benchmark.

### 4.2 Through-Cycle Normalization

- Normalize NIM for a more typical deposit beta and yield-curve shape.
- Normalize credit costs using portfolio history, not only current benign loss rates.
- Treat reserve releases, unusually low funding costs, and temporary fee booms as non-recurring support.
- For acquisition-driven banks, normalize for integration costs and realistic expense saves rather than management targets alone.

### 4.3 Structural Features and Creditor Protections

- Holding-company creditors are structurally subordinated to operating-bank depositors and operating liabilities.
- Upstream dividend capacity is constrained by regulators and capital thresholds.
- Preferred stock, TLAC-style instruments, and subordinated debt can change loss absorption and resolution outcomes.
- Resolution and ring-fencing frameworks matter more for banks than for most sectors.

### 4.4 EBITDA Addback and Adjustment Quality

Banks do not use classic EBITDA adjustments, but analogous distortions still matter.

- Provision normalization can be reasonable or abusive depending on cycle position.
- Fair-value exclusions and securities accounting can hide real economic pressure.
- Cost-save stories from M&A often arrive slower and at higher regulatory and integration cost than projected.
- Management-adjusted "core" earnings should be reconciled back to reserve movements, hedging gains/losses, and securities repositioning.

## Dimension 5: External and Systemic Risk

### 5.1 Regulatory and Legal Environment

- Bank regulation is a core part of the credit, not a side constraint.
- Capital, liquidity, consumer-compliance, BSA/AML, fair-lending, and stress-testing regimes can all change business economics materially.
- Consent orders, MRAs, and supervisory criticism are major warning signs even before reported earnings weaken.
- Resolution expectations differ sharply by size and systemic importance.

### 5.2 Technology Disruption and Secular Displacement

- Digital-first banks, payments platforms, and embedded-finance models pressure fee pools and deposit gathering.
- Open banking, real-time payments, and automation increase required technology spending.
- Banks with weak digital offerings can lose low-cost operating accounts even if loan demand remains intact.
- AI can improve underwriting and efficiency, but it also raises model-risk, fraud, and compliance questions.

### 5.3 Environmental, Climate, and ESG Exposure

- Physical climate risk matters through insured collateral, CRE, agriculture, and municipal exposures.
- Transition risk affects sector concentrations in energy, transportation, heavy industry, and vulnerable geographies.
- Governance failures in credit culture, risk management, or incentives often precede visible asset-quality problems.
- Community-investment, fair-lending, and reputational issues can affect both franchise strength and regulatory posture.

### 5.4 Common Underwriting Traps and Sector-Specific Red Flags

- Rapid asset growth without commensurate reserve, capital, or funding strengthening
- Deposit growth concentrated in uninsured, sponsor, or sector-correlated accounts
- Office CRE or construction concentration masked inside broad CRE disclosure
- NIM supported by temporarily low deposit beta that is already normalizing upward
- AOCI or securities losses treated as irrelevant despite liquidity and confidence implications
- Reserve ratios falling while early delinquencies and criticized assets rise
- Heavy reliance on wholesale funding presented as "diversification"
- Management turnover in credit, risk, treasury, or compliance functions

## Handoff to Credit Modeling

For `modeling-and-valuation`, model banks around earnings absorption and funding resilience rather than generic EBITDA stress:

- Stress deposit costs, deposit runoff, and mix shift alongside loan repricing.
- Apply portfolio-specific loss scenarios by major book (CRE, C&I, cards, indirect auto, mortgage, etc.).
- Test PPNR, reserve build, CET1 depletion, and liquidity survival together rather than separately.
- Include a confidence stress where funding access tightens before charge-offs fully emerge.
