# Energy & Midstream Credit Analysis

## Sector Overview

The energy sector spans upstream exploration and production (E&P), midstream gathering/processing/transportation, downstream refining, and oilfield services. Midstream has become the preferred energy sub-sector for credit investors due to its fee-based, contracted revenue model that reduces direct commodity exposure. The sector is undergoing a secular transformation driven by ESG considerations, the energy transition, and the maturation of US shale production. Total US oil production exceeds 13 million barrels per day, and natural gas production exceeds 100 Bcf/d, with the Permian Basin accounting for approximately 40% of US oil output.

Market structure varies: E&P is fragmented with hundreds of operators; midstream has consolidated significantly (top 10 operators control roughly 60% of long-haul pipeline capacity); refining is concentrated among 5-7 major operators; and oilfield services is dominated by three large-cap companies with a long tail of smaller specialists.

## Dimension 1: Cash Flow Quality and Predictability

### 1.1 Revenue / Income Visibility

Revenue visibility varies dramatically across energy sub-segments, from near-utility contracted midstream to fully spot-exposed E&P:

**E&P:**
- Revenue resets continuously with commodity prices. WTI crude, Henry Hub natural gas, and NGL prices directly drive revenue. OPEC+ production decisions, global demand (China), and US inventory levels are key price drivers.
- **Hedging program**: Percentage of production hedged and at what prices for the next 12-24 months. A well-managed E&P hedges 50-75% of near-term production at prices above breakeven. Hedging locks in cash flow but caps upside. Evaluate hedge book mark-to-market, hedge tenor, and instruments used (swaps provide certainty; collars provide floors with upside participation).
- **PV-10 (present value of proved reserves at 10% discount rate)**: The primary asset value metric. Debt/PV-10 below 25% is conservative; above 40% is aggressive. Strip pricing vs SEC pricing produces materially different PV-10 values.

**Midstream:**
- **Contract quality**: Percentage of revenue under fee-based, take-or-pay, or minimum volume commitment (MVC) contracts. Fee-based revenue above 80% is strong; commodity-sensitive revenue above 30% introduces meaningful price risk.
- **Average contract duration**: Weighted average remaining contract life. Above 5 years provides strong visibility; below 3 years creates recontracting risk.
- **Gathering and processing fees**: Midstream revenue driven by throughput volumes and contractual fee escalators (typically CPI-based or fixed 2-3% annual increases).
- **Pipeline tariff structures**: FERC-regulated interstate pipelines have tariff-based revenue with inflation escalators. Intrastate pipelines have more negotiated/market-based rates.

**Refining:**
- Minimal forward revenue visibility. Revenue depends on crack spreads (difference between refined product prices and crude oil input costs) that reset continuously. Margins driven by supply-demand balance for refined products, crude oil differentials, and regulatory factors (RINs for biofuel compliance, sulfur specifications).

**Renewable energy:**
- Contracted revenue (PPAs, offtake agreements) supports visibility. Tax equity structures add complexity. Intermittency risk and resource assessment accuracy matter for solar and wind.

*Analytical questions:*

- *Separate commodity-linked cash flows from fee-based or contracted revenue when mapping leverage to rating — midstream and E&P are not interchangeable.*
- *For E&P, stress reserves, hedging tenor, and basin concentration against the price deck used in the analysis, not just spot narratives.*
- *For midstream, how strong are MVCs, recontracting risk, and counterparty quality if producer activity or credit deteriorates?*

### 1.2 Cyclicality and Macro Sensitivity

Energy is among the most cyclical sectors in credit, though sub-segment exposure varies enormously:

- **E&P**: Among the most cyclical credits in any sector. EBITDA can swing 40-60% with a $20/bbl move in WTI. Even hedged companies face cliff risk when hedges roll off at lower prices. Gas-weighted E&Ps face additional basis differential risk.
- **Commodity prices**: OPEC+ production decisions, global demand (China), and US inventory levels are key price drivers.
- **Midstream**: Moderate cyclicality. Volume risk is the primary sensitivity rather than direct commodity price exposure. Fee-based contracts with MVCs dampen cyclicality but do not eliminate it — volumes consistently at MVCs may signal producer stress.
- **Refining**: Cyclical with limited ability to influence margins. Crack spreads driven by supply-demand balance for refined products, crude oil differentials, and regulatory factors (RINs for biofuel compliance, sulfur specifications). Gulf Coast 3-2-1 crack spread of $15-20/bbl is mid-cycle; below $10 is stressed.
- **Oilfield services**: Most leveraged to E&P capital spending. Revenue visibility limited to 6-12 months. Pricing power depends on utilization; when rig counts fall, pricing deteriorates rapidly.
- **Production volumes and basin economics**: Permian, Appalachia (Marcellus/Utica), Haynesville, DJ Basin, and Eagle Ford are the key US production basins. Breakeven economics vary by basin and operator: Permian breakevens typically $35-45/bbl WTI; Appalachian gas $2.00-2.50/MMBtu.

*Analytical questions:*

- *For refining and oilfield services, is margin and utilization sensitivity to the cycle explicit in liquidity and covenant headroom?*
- *For renewables, are offtake, curtailment, and tax-equity or financing dependencies consistent with a through-cycle view?*

### 1.3 Seasonality and Cash Flow Timing

- **Natural gas**: Seasonal demand pattern with winter heating season driving higher prices and volumes (November-March). Summer cooling demand provides secondary peak.
- **Refining**: Seasonal gasoline demand peaks in Q2-Q3 (driving season). Turnaround maintenance is typically scheduled for Q1 and Q4 when demand is lower. Refining margins have seasonal patterns tied to driving season (gasoline) and winter (heating oil/distillates).
- **E&P completion activity**: Historically concentrated in spring and fall; weather constraints in certain basins (Bakken winter, Haynesville summer).
- **Midstream distributions**: Quarterly distribution payments create predictable cash outflow timing. Coverage ratios must account for distribution cadence.
- **Working capital**: Working capital can swing dramatically with crude price movements, particularly for refining. Hedging locks in cash flow but caps upside; evaluate hedge book roll-off timing and its impact on cash flow variability.

### 1.4 Concentration Risk

Concentration risk in energy manifests through geographic, commodity, and counterparty dimensions:

- **Basin concentration (E&P)**: Single-basin operators face geographic concentration risk. Permian operators face additional risks from water disposal constraints and seismicity regulation.
- **Counterparty credit risk (midstream)**: Midstream companies are only as strong as their shipper/producer counterparts. If anchor producers go bankrupt, contracts may be rejected. Evaluate counterparty creditworthiness and geographic diversification. Top 3 shippers representing more than 50% of midstream revenue is a concentration concern.
- **Volume throughput trends**: Actual volumes vs MVCs indicate producer health and basin activity. Volumes consistently at MVCs may signal producer stress; volumes well above MVCs indicate upside.
- **Commodity concentration**: Gas-weighted E&Ps face additional challenges from persistently low natural gas prices and basis differential risk. Increasing differentials between realized prices and benchmark commodity prices signal concentration or quality risk.
- **Refinery concentration**: Single-refinery operators face concentrated operational and geographic risk. Gulf Coast concentration creates hurricane exposure.

## Dimension 2: Margin and Cost Structure Resilience

### 2.1 Pricing Power and Rate-Setting Ability

Pricing power varies dramatically across the energy value chain:

- **E&P**: Pure price-taker. Commodity prices determined by global supply-demand balances entirely outside any single company's control.
- **Midstream**: Moderate pricing power through contracted fee structures. FERC-regulated interstate pipelines have tariff-based pricing with inflation escalators. Unregulated gathering and processing has more negotiated pricing but is subject to recontracting risk at contract maturity. Contractual fee escalators (typically CPI-based or fixed 2-3% annual increases) provide inflation protection.
- **Refining**: No pricing power over crack spreads. Advantaged refineries (Gulf Coast complex refiners with high Nelson Complexity Index, above 12) have more stable profitability than simple refineries by being able to process cheaper heavy/sour crude, generating higher margins.
- **Oilfield services**: Pricing power depends on utilization. Technology differentiation (rotary steerable, pressure pumping efficiency) provides competitive moat and pricing premium.
- **Renewables**: Contracted revenue (PPAs) provides price certainty for the contract term. Recontracting risk at PPA expiration.

### 2.2 Operating Leverage and Cost Flexibility

- **E&P**: Mixed cost structure. Significant variable costs (royalties, production taxes, LOE) decline with production, but base infrastructure costs are relatively fixed. Higher decline-rate wells (shale) have more variable cost profiles than conventional production. **Production decline rate**: Base decline rate (how fast existing wells decline without new drilling). Shale wells decline 60-70% in year one, 30-40% in year two. Higher decline rates mean more capital required to maintain production. **Reinvestment rate**: Capex as percentage of operating cash flow. Rates below 60% indicate ability to generate meaningful free cash flow; above 80% leaves limited margin.
- **Midstream**: High fixed-cost infrastructure (pipelines, processing plants, compression). Operating leverage is significant — volume declines below a threshold rapidly compress margins. However, MVCs and take-or-pay provisions provide a contractual floor.
- **Refining**: High fixed costs. Refinery utilization rate directly drives profitability. Sustained rates above 90% indicate strong demand; below 85% signals oversupply or operational issues. Nelson Complexity Index above 12 is advantageous, enabling processing of cheaper heavy/sour crude.
- **Oilfield services**: More variable cost structure with ability to stack equipment and reduce crews, but at significant revenue cost.

### 2.3 Input Cost and Inflation Exposure

- **E&P**: Production costs (LOE) include labor, energy, chemicals, and well maintenance. **Finding and development costs (F&D)**: All-in cost to add a barrel of oil equivalent reserves. F&D costs should be well below expected commodity prices for the business to sustain itself. Service cost inflation (rigs, completion crews, sand, water) directly impacts E&P economics.
- **Midstream**: Electricity and compression costs are the primary variable operating inputs. Labor for field operations. Pipeline integrity maintenance costs.
- **Refining**: Crude oil is the dominant input. Working capital can swing dramatically with crude price movements. Biofuel mandates (RFS, SAF) create compliance costs. RINs for biofuel compliance and sulfur specifications are growing regulatory input costs.
- **Decommissioning and environmental liabilities**: Plugging and abandonment obligations for E&P; pipeline integrity and remediation for midstream. These can be material for mature asset bases.

### 2.4 Through-Cycle Margin Stability

- **E&P**: Wide margin swings. EBITDA margins highly correlated with commodity prices. EBITDA can swing 40-60% with a $20/bbl move in WTI. Hedging compresses the range but does not eliminate volatility.
- **Midstream**: Most stable margins in the energy sector due to fee-based contracts. DCF margins relatively predictable when contract quality is high. Both rating agencies adjust midstream EBITDA for commodity-sensitive income, applying haircuts to non-contracted revenue.
- **Refining**: Margin range tied to crack spreads. Gulf Coast 3-2-1 crack spread of $15-20/bbl is mid-cycle; below $10 is stressed. Complex refineries outperform simple refineries across the cycle.
- **Oilfield services**: Margins highly correlated with rig count and utilization. Pricing deteriorates rapidly when rig counts fall.

## Dimension 3: Capital Requirements and Asset Profile

### 3.1 Capital Expenditure / Investment Intensity

Capital intensity is high across most energy sub-segments:

**E&P:**
- **Reinvestment rate**: Capex as percentage of operating cash flow. Rates below 60% indicate ability to generate meaningful free cash flow; above 80% leaves limited margin.
- **Reserve replacement**: E&P companies must continuously invest to replace produced reserves. Failure to replace reserves at economic costs leads to a declining asset base. Reserve life (proved reserves / annual production) below 7 years is thin; above 12 years is conservative. Proved developed (PD) reserve life is more relevant than total proved.
- **Sustaining vs growth**: Maintenance capex maintains existing well production; growth capex drills new wells and develops new acreage.

**Midstream:**
- Capital-intensive to build new pipeline and processing infrastructure, but once built, maintenance capex is relatively modest.
- Growth capex self-funding ability is a rating agency focus. Evaluate whether growth capex is funded from operating cash flow, debt, or equity.

**Refining:**
- Periodic turnaround maintenance (every 4-5 years per unit). Regulatory-driven capex (Tier 3 gasoline, IMO 2020) can be significant and non-deferrable. Biofuel mandates (RFS, SAF) creating both capex costs and opportunities.

**Oilfield services:**
- Capital-intensive equipment fleet (rigs, pressure pumping, wireline).

**Renewables:**
- High upfront capital with low ongoing maintenance capex. Tax equity structures provide construction-phase financing. Battery storage integration emerging as a critical differentiator.

### 3.2 Working Capital and Liquidity Demands

- **E&P**: Moderate working capital needs. Revenue is collected relatively quickly from purchasers. Hedging collateral requirements can create liquidity demands during commodity price spikes (margin calls on out-of-the-money hedges). Reserve-based lending (RBL) is the primary revolver, with borrowing base redeterminations semi-annually based on reserve engineering. Borrowing base reductions in falling commodity environments can create acute liquidity stress.
- **Midstream**: Low working capital intensity for fee-based operations. Commodity-exposed midstream (marketing, NGL) has higher working capital volatility. **Distributable cash flow (DCF) coverage**: DCF / total distributions. Coverage above 1.3x provides comfortable distribution sustainability. Below 1.1x raises distribution cut risk.
- **Refining**: Very high working capital intensity. Crude oil inventory (typically 30-45 days) creates large working capital requirements. Crude price swings drive dramatic working capital movements.

### 3.3 Asset Quality, Tangibility, and Liquidation Value

- **E&P**: Reserves are the primary asset. **PV-10** is the primary asset value metric. Debt/PV-10 below 25% is conservative; above 40% is aggressive. Oil-weighted reserves generally have higher value than gas-weighted. Basin quality and breakeven economics determine asset value. Strip pricing vs SEC pricing produces materially different PV-10 values.
- **Midstream**: Pipeline assets have scarcity value and significant replacement cost, supporting recovery analysis. Gathering and processing assets have more volume risk than long-haul pipelines but lower scarcity value. Pipeline rights-of-way are difficult to replicate and provide durable competitive advantage.
- **Refining**: Refinery replacement cost is substantial ($10B+ for a complex refinery). However, environmental liabilities and decommissioning costs offset some asset value.
- **Renewables**: Solar and wind farm assets have tangible value but limited alternative use. PPA contracts contribute to going-concern value.

### 3.4 Useful Life, Obsolescence, and Stranded Asset Risk

- **E&P**: Reserves are depleting assets by definition. Without continuous investment in exploration and development, production declines and the company shrinks. **Reserve life** (proved reserves / annual production) below 7 years is thin; above 12 years is conservative. Reserve replacement costs have risen as easily accessible deposits have been developed. Stranded asset risk for long-lived reserves in an energy transition scenario.
- **Midstream**: Pipelines have 40-60+ year useful lives but face energy transition stranded asset risk. Natural gas infrastructure has longer secular runway than crude oil pipelines. Some gathering systems may become stranded if basin economics deteriorate.
- **Refining**: Refineries can operate 50+ years with maintenance. Biofuel conversion creates optionality. ICE vehicle fleet decline creates long-term demand headwind.
- **Oilfield services**: Equipment has moderate useful life (5-15 years). Technology obsolescence risk for older equipment types.
- **Renewables**: Solar panels 25-30 years; wind turbines 20-25 years. Recontracting risk at end of PPA term. Technology improvement may make older installations less competitive.
- **Decommissioning and environmental liabilities**: Plugging and abandonment obligations for E&P; pipeline integrity and remediation for midstream. These can be material for mature asset bases.

## Dimension 4: Structural Protection and Leverage Capacity

### 4.1 Leverage and Coverage Calibration

Leverage tolerance varies enormously across energy sub-segments due to differences in cash flow volatility:

- **E&P**: Conservative leverage required due to commodity exposure. Debt/PV-10 below 25% is conservative; above 40% is aggressive. Debt/EBITDA should be evaluated at mid-cycle pricing, not current spot.
- **Midstream**: Moderate leverage tolerance (4.0-5.0x Debt/EBITDA typical for IG-rated). Fee-based contract quality directly influences leverage capacity — higher quality contracts support higher leverage. DCF coverage above 1.3x provides comfortable distribution sustainability; below 1.1x raises distribution cut risk.
- **Refining**: Low leverage tolerance due to cyclical margins. Conservative operators maintain net cash or very low leverage.
- **Oilfield services**: Low leverage tolerance. Highly cyclical cash flows require conservative balance sheets.

### 4.2 Through-Cycle Normalization

- **E&P**: Rating agencies use standardized commodity price assumptions (price decks) rather than forward strip. S&P publishes annual price decks that are deliberately conservative. Leverage must be evaluated at trough pricing scenarios, not just current strip. PV-10 varies significantly with price deck assumptions — SEC pricing uses trailing 12-month average; strip pricing reflects forward market; agency pricing uses mid-cycle assumptions. Each produces materially different reserve values.
- **Midstream**: Normalize for commodity-sensitive income. Rating agencies adjust midstream EBITDA for commodity-sensitive income, applying haircuts to non-contracted revenue. They also evaluate midstream exposure to volume risk from producer curtailments during low commodity price periods. Volume throughput trends vs MVCs — volumes consistently at MVCs may signal producer stress; volumes well above MVCs indicate upside. Growing DUC (drilled uncompleted) well inventory at the E&P customer level signals reduced completion activity and potential future volume declines.
- **Refining**: Mid-cycle crack spread assumptions should drive base case. Current spreads above $20/bbl should be treated as peak, not sustainable.

### 4.3 Structural Features and Creditor Protections

Energy credits feature several sector-specific structural considerations:

- **Reserve-based lending (RBL)**: E&P companies typically have an RBL as their primary revolver, with borrowing base redeterminations semi-annually based on reserve engineering. Borrowing base reductions in falling commodity environments can create acute liquidity stress.
- **Commodity hedging requirements**: Some credit agreements require minimum hedging levels (e.g., 50% of PDP production for 12 months). Evaluate whether hedging covenants are sufficient to protect cash flow.
- **MLP-specific provisions**: Legacy MLP structures may have distribution covenants or incentive distribution rights that create conflicts between equity holders and creditors. IDR simplification transactions can affect cash flow available for debt service.
- **Asset coverage tests**: E&P and midstream loans may include asset coverage ratios (debt/PV-10 or debt/appraised value). These provide creditor protection but can trigger defaults when commodity prices fall, even if cash flow remains adequate.
- **Capital expenditure limitations**: Some E&P agreements cap capex as a percentage of cash flow, preventing overleveraging through growth spending.

### 4.4 EBITDA Addback and Adjustment Quality

- **Hedging gains/losses**: Evaluate whether realized and unrealized hedge gains/losses are included in or excluded from adjusted EBITDA, and whether treatment is symmetric.
- **Commodity-sensitive midstream income**: Rating agencies haircut non-contracted revenue; evaluate whether credit agreement EBITDA definitions do the same. Both S&P and Moody's adjust midstream EBITDA for commodity-sensitive income, applying haircuts to non-contracted revenue.
- **Exploration and abandonment costs**: E&P companies may exclude dry hole costs, impairments, and abandonment charges from adjusted EBITDA. Evaluate whether these are genuinely non-recurring.
- **Pro forma for acquisitions**: Energy sector M&A is frequent. Assess whether pro forma EBITDA for acquisitions reflects realistic run-rate synergies and integration assumptions.
- **Management prioritizing production growth or distribution growth** over balance sheet deleveraging inflates near-term EBITDA but increases risk.

## Dimension 5: External and Systemic Risk

### 5.1 Regulatory and Legal Environment

- **Pipeline permitting**: Pipeline permitting has become significantly more difficult (environmental reviews, eminent domain challenges). This creates scarcity value for existing infrastructure but limits growth.
- **LNG export terminal approvals**: Face political headwinds. Approval delays can strand contracted capacity.
- **FERC rate cases**: FERC rate case outcomes affect pipeline tariffs and midstream revenue for interstate pipelines.
- **Basin concentration regulatory risk**: Permian operators face additional risks from water disposal constraints and seismicity regulation.
- **Environmental compliance**: Emissions regulations, methane detection requirements, water discharge standards. EPA and state-level enforcement.
- **E&P regulatory**: Drilling permits, flaring restrictions, methane emission requirements, and federal land lease policies all affect E&P operations and costs.

### 5.2 Technology Disruption and Secular Displacement

- **Energy transition**: The overarching secular risk for the sector. Electric vehicle adoption reduces petroleum demand over the long term. Renewable energy displaces natural gas in power generation. Timeline and pace are uncertain but direction is clear.
- **Battery storage**: Emerging as a critical differentiator for renewable energy. Reduces intermittency risk and improves grid integration. Potentially displaces natural gas peaker plants.
- **Hydrogen**: Potential long-term displacement of natural gas in certain applications (industrial heat, heavy transport). Infrastructure repurposing potential for existing pipelines.
- **Carbon capture**: Could extend the economic life of fossil fuel infrastructure if commercialized at scale.
- **Shale technology**: Continued efficiency gains in drilling and completion reduce E&P breakevens but also accelerate resource depletion in mature basins.
- **Oilfield services technology**: Technology differentiation (rotary steerable, pressure pumping efficiency) provides competitive moat.

### 5.3 Environmental, Climate, and ESG Exposure

- **ESG-driven capital restrictions**: Banks and institutional investors restricting fossil fuel exposure can increase cost of capital. This disproportionately affects smaller, less diversified operators. Carbon pricing legislation would disproportionately affect coal and higher-emission operations.
- **Stranded asset risk**: Long-lived reserves and infrastructure face potential stranding as energy transition progresses.
- **Methane emissions**: Increasingly regulated and monitored. Methane intensity metrics becoming standard for ESG-focused investors.
- **Physical climate risk**: Gulf Coast infrastructure exposed to hurricane risk. Wildfire risk for western operations.
- **Decommissioning and environmental liabilities**: Plugging and abandonment obligations for E&P; pipeline integrity and remediation for midstream. Significant unfunded decommissioning or environmental remediation obligations are a red flag.
- **Pipeline integrity incidents or regulatory citations** signal operational and environmental risk.

### 5.4 Common Underwriting Traps and Red Flags

**Sector-specific underwriting traps:**

- **Conflating midstream with E&P in leverage analysis**: Fee-based midstream and commodity-exposed E&P have fundamentally different risk profiles and leverage tolerance — they are not interchangeable.
- **Underwriting E&P at current strip pricing**: Stress reserves, hedging tenor, and basin concentration against a through-cycle price deck, not just spot narratives.
- **Treating midstream MVC floors as guaranteed revenue**: Must assess counterparty credit quality and contract enforceability in bankruptcy.
- **Assuming current capacity constraints persist**: High utilization may not persist through the full debt tenor without considering new capacity additions.
- **Ignoring recontracting risk for midstream**: Contracted revenue provides visibility only for the current contract term; renewal rates and competitive dynamics matter.

**Red flags:**

- E&P reserve replacement ratio consistently below 100% without corresponding leverage reduction
- Hedging program covering less than 40% of near-term production at prices below breakeven
- Midstream volume throughput trending toward MVC floors across multiple systems
- Counterparty concentration where top 3 shippers represent more than 50% of midstream revenue
- Capex guidance repeatedly increasing without corresponding production or volume growth
- Increasing differentials between realized prices and benchmark commodity prices
- Growing DUC (drilled uncompleted) well inventory at the E&P customer level signaling reduced completion activity
- Management prioritizing production growth or distribution growth over balance sheet deleveraging
- Significant unfunded decommissioning or environmental remediation obligations
- Pipeline integrity incidents or regulatory citations

## Sub-Segments: Key Distinctions

- **E&P (exploration & production)**: Highest commodity sensitivity. Evaluate basin quality, reserve depth, cost structure, and hedging program. Permian operators generally have the best economics and access to capital. Gas-weighted operators face additional challenges from persistently low natural gas prices.
- **Midstream/MLPs**: Preferred energy sub-sector for credit. Fee-based contracts provide revenue stability but volume risk remains. Gathering and processing assets have more volume risk than long-haul pipelines. Pipeline assets have scarcity value and significant replacement cost, supporting recovery analysis.
- **Refining**: Cyclical with limited ability to influence margins. Advantaged refineries (Gulf Coast complex refiners) have more stable profitability than simple refineries. Biofuel mandates (RFS, SAF) creating both costs and opportunities. Working capital can swing dramatically with crude price movements.
- **Oilfield services**: Most leveraged to E&P capital spending. Revenue visibility limited to 6-12 months. Pricing power depends on utilization; when rig counts fall, pricing deteriorates rapidly. Technology differentiation (rotary steerable, pressure pumping efficiency) provides competitive moat.
- **Renewable energy developers**: Contracted revenue (PPAs, offtake agreements) supports leverage. Tax equity structures add complexity. Intermittency risk and resource assessment accuracy matter for solar and wind. Battery storage integration emerging as a critical differentiator.

## Rating Agency Focus Areas

**S&P**: Uses standardized commodity price assumptions (price decks) rather than forward strip to evaluate E&P credits. Emphasizes debt/proved developed reserves, all-in cost structures, and hedging effectiveness. For midstream, focuses on contract quality and counterparty concentration. Assigns business risk profiles that explicitly reflect commodity exposure.

**Moody's**: Focuses on leveraged full-cycle ratio (LFCR) for E&P, which measures whether the company generates sufficient cash to cover all costs including finding and development costs through a full commodity cycle. For midstream, evaluates distribution coverage, contract recontracting risk, and growth capex self-funding ability. Explicitly considers governance quality, including financial policy discipline regarding shareholder returns vs debt reduction.

Both agencies adjust midstream EBITDA for commodity-sensitive income, applying haircuts to non-contracted revenue. They also evaluate midstream exposure to volume risk from producer curtailments during low commodity price periods.
