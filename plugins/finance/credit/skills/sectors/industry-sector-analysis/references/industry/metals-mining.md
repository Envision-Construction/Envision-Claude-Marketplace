# Metals & Mining Credit Analysis

## Sector Overview

The metals and mining sector is among the most cyclical and capital-intensive in credit markets. The US steel industry generates approximately $150 billion in annual revenue, with aluminum, copper, gold, coal, iron ore, and other base and precious metals adding significantly to the broader sector. Market structure is consolidated at the top, with large diversified miners (BHP, Rio Tinto, Glencore, Vale) controlling vast multi-commodity portfolios, while the mid-cap and junior tiers remain fragmented with hundreds of single-asset or single-commodity operators that represent the majority of high-yield credit exposure.

Chinese demand is the dominant driver of global pricing for most industrial metals and bulk commodities, with China consuming approximately 50% of global copper, 55% of aluminum, and 60% of steel. US steel producers benefit from structural trade protection under Section 232 tariffs (25% on steel imports, 10% on aluminum), which has provided domestic pricing support since 2018. The sector is undergoing a secular shift driven by the energy transition, with battery metals (lithium, cobalt, nickel) and copper experiencing demand growth from EV production and grid electrification, while thermal coal faces structural decline from displacement by natural gas and renewables.

## Dimension 1: Cash Flow Quality and Predictability

### 1.1 Revenue / Income Visibility

Metals and mining sits at the lowest end of the revenue visibility spectrum — nearly all revenue is spot or transactional:

- **Commodity prices**: The overwhelmingly dominant revenue driver. Commodity prices are determined by global supply-demand balances largely outside any single company's control. Gold: driven by real interest rates, USD strength, and safe-haven demand. Copper: driven by Chinese construction, EV production, and mine supply constraints. Steel: driven by construction activity, manufacturing, and trade policy.
- **Production volumes**: Tonnes mined and processed determine revenue at prevailing prices. Production disruptions (equipment failure, weather, labor actions) have immediate revenue impact. Production guidance and achievement rates are closely monitored.
- **Hedging percentage**: Portion of near-term production sold forward at fixed prices. Gold miners commonly hedge 10-30% of production; base metal producers less frequently. Hedging provides cash flow certainty but caps upside.
- **Offtake agreements**: Contracted sales to smelters, traders, or end-users provide some revenue certainty but pricing is typically index-linked rather than fixed, providing volume certainty without price certainty.
- **Inventory cycles**: Steel service center and end-user inventory levels drive short-term demand swings above or below underlying consumption. Destocking cycles can depress apparent demand by 10-15% below real consumption; restocking cycles amplify demand by a similar magnitude.

**Analytical questions:**
- Is cost-curve position and reserve or resource life strong enough that leverage is judged through a commodity down-cycle, not the current price?
- Does the commodity mix (bulk, precious, battery metals) imply different volatility than a single headline leverage band suggests?

### 1.2 Cyclicality and Macro Sensitivity

Metals and mining is among the most cyclical sectors in credit markets:

- **Commodity price cyclicality**: The most volatile major sector in credit markets. EBITDA swings of 50-80% peak-to-trough are common. Steel EBITDA can decline 60%+ when hot-rolled coil prices fall from $1,200/ton to $600/ton. Copper producers face similar magnitude swings. Leverage sized at peak prices becomes unsustainable at trough.
- **Commodity price sensitivity**: EBITDA impact of +/-20% commodity price moves. This analysis reveals the degree of operating leverage and the price level at which cash flow turns negative.
- **Global demand dynamics**: Chinese infrastructure and property sector activity drives demand for steel, iron ore, and copper. Indian industrialization is an emerging demand driver. Global EV production is driving secular demand growth for copper, lithium, cobalt, and nickel.
- **Trade policy and tariffs**: Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum provide US domestic price support of $100-200/ton above global prices. Anti-dumping duties on specific products from specific countries affect competitive dynamics. Trade policy changes can rapidly alter domestic pricing.
- **Capacity utilization**: Steel mills: above 80% supports pricing; below 75% indicates oversupply. Mines: utilization below design capacity may signal operational issues or demand constraints.

**Analytical questions:**
- For steel and base metals, is utilization and capacity additions in the industry part of the stress narrative?
- Is the current price above, at, or below mid-cycle — and is leverage underwritten to normalized, not current, pricing?

### 1.3 Seasonality and Cash Flow Timing

- **Construction-driven demand**: Steel and copper demand follow construction activity patterns — Q2-Q3 peak in the Northern Hemisphere.
- **Weather impacts on production**: Open-pit mining operations can be affected by rainy seasons (tropical mines), extreme cold (Canadian/Russian operations), and other seasonal weather patterns.
- **Turnaround and maintenance scheduling**: Steel mill and smelter maintenance typically scheduled during lower-demand periods.
- **Capital spending timing**: Growth capex for mine development follows project milestones rather than seasonal patterns, but can create lumpy cash flow profiles.
- **Cash sweep mechanisms**: Credit agreements may accelerate debt repayment during high-price periods, concentrating free cash flow in specific quarters.

### 1.4 Concentration Risk

- **Single-asset or single-commodity concentration**: Representing more than 70% of EBITDA creates acute concentration risk. The majority of high-yield mining credits are single-asset or single-commodity operators.
- **Geographic concentration**: Mining assets are immovable and subject to host country regulatory, tax, and political risk. Jurisdictional quality varies dramatically: Australia and Canada are low-risk; DRC, Zambia, and certain Latin American countries carry elevated political risk.
- **Chinese demand concentration**: China consuming 50-60% of global production for most industrial metals creates macro concentration risk for the entire sector.
- **Customer concentration**: Less material for commodity producers selling into deep liquid markets, but significant for specialty steel and niche metal producers.

**Analytical question:**
- How does jurisdiction, royalty, tax, and resource-nationalism risk change the range of outcomes versus peers at similar leverage?

## Dimension 2: Margin and Cost Structure Resilience

### 2.1 Pricing Power and Rate-Setting Ability

Metals and mining companies are fundamentally price-takers:

- **No pricing power**: Commodity prices determined by global supply-demand balances entirely outside any single company's control. Cost management is the primary lever for maintaining profitability through cycles.
- **Trade protection**: US steel producers benefit from Section 232 tariffs providing $100-200/ton domestic price support. This is a regulatory advantage, not intrinsic pricing power, and is subject to policy change.
- **Quality premiums**: Higher-grade iron ore, premium steel alloys, and specialty metals command pricing premiums relative to benchmark grades. Quality differentials (iron content, impurities) create pricing tiers that favor higher-grade producers.
- **Currency effects**: Mining costs are incurred in local currency (Australian dollar, Canadian dollar, Chilean peso, South African rand) while commodity revenue is denominated in US dollars. A weaker local currency improves margins for non-US miners; a stronger USD benefits US-based producers.

### 2.2 Operating Leverage and Cost Flexibility

- **High operating leverage**: Mining and steel production have high fixed-cost bases (equipment, infrastructure, labor in remote locations). EBITDA declines faster than revenue when commodity prices fall.
- **Cost management as the primary lever**: Given limited pricing power, cost discipline is the primary lever for maintaining profitability through cycles. Labor, energy, consumables (explosives, grinding media, reagents), and contractor costs are the major components.
- **Cash cost per unit of production**: The fundamental profitability metric. $/ton for steel (hot-rolled coil benchmark), $/lb for copper, $/oz for gold, $/ton for coal. Cash costs determine survival during price troughs — operators above mid-cycle price on the cost curve face existential risk.
- **All-in sustaining cost (AISC)**: Includes cash costs plus sustaining capital, exploration, and reclamation amortization. AISC represents the true cost of maintaining current production rates. AISC below the 10-year average commodity price indicates through-cycle viability.
- **Cost curve position**: Ranking relative to global competitors (1st through 4th quartile). First-quartile producers survive all but the most extreme downturns; 4th-quartile producers are price-takers with minimal margin cushion.
- **Steel sub-segment differences**: Integrated mills (blast furnace) have higher fixed costs and greater sensitivity to iron ore and coking coal prices. EAF/mini-mills (Nucor, Steel Dynamics) use scrap steel feedstock, have lower fixed costs, and can flex production more rapidly.

### 2.3 Input Cost and Inflation Exposure

- **Energy costs**: Mining is energy-intensive. Electricity costs are critical for aluminum smelting (creates natural advantage for producers with low-cost hydro power). Diesel for haulage equipment. Natural gas or coking coal for steel production.
- **Labor**: Mining operations often depend on specialized workforces in remote locations. Union activity, strikes, and work stoppages can halt production. Safety incidents trigger regulatory shutdowns. Skills shortages in certain mining jurisdictions increase labor costs.
- **Consumables**: Explosives, grinding media, reagents, and replacement parts represent significant ongoing costs.
- **Foreign exchange**: A 10% appreciation in the Australian dollar can reduce an Australian miner's EBITDA margin by 300-500 basis points. Non-USD cost structures create margin volatility when currencies move.
- **Ore grade quality**: Average grade of ore being mined relative to reserve grades. Declining head grades increase processing costs and reduce recovery rates. Grade is the single most important determinant of mine-level economics. Rising strip ratios (waste-to-ore) in open-pit operations increase unit costs.

### 2.4 Through-Cycle Margin Stability

- **Extreme margin volatility**: Metals and mining has the widest margin range of any major credit sector. EBITDA margins can swing from 40%+ at peak to negative at trough for commodity producers.
- **Cost curve position as margin anchor**: First-quartile producers maintain positive margins through most commodity environments. Fourth-quartile producers have negative margins at any point below mid-cycle pricing.
- **Diversification benefit**: Diversified miners with multiple commodities have more stable aggregate margins than single-commodity operators, as commodity cycles are not perfectly correlated.
- **Gold counter-cyclicality**: Gold serves as an inflation hedge and safe-haven asset, providing some counter-cyclical demand characteristics that can partially offset base metal margin compression.

## Dimension 3: Capital Requirements and Asset Profile

### 3.1 Capital Expenditure / Investment Intensity

- **Capital intensity**: New mine development costs $500M-$5B+ and takes 5-10 years from discovery to production. Construction cost overruns are common (30-50% above initial estimates is not unusual). Expansion capex during high commodity prices often delivers into weaker markets.
- **Sustaining vs growth capex**: Sustaining capex maintains current operations; growth capex expands capacity or develops new projects. High growth capex relative to sustaining capex increases execution risk and cash consumption.
- **Reserve depletion**: Mines are depleting assets. Without continuous investment in exploration and development, production declines and the company shrinks. Reserve replacement costs have risen significantly as easily accessible deposits have been developed.
- **Production profile**: Whether production is growing, stable, or declining. Declining production without offsetting cost improvements or new projects signals deteriorating asset quality.

**Analytical questions:**
- Are sustaining capex, closure, and reclamation treated as ongoing cash needs tied to the operating plan?
- Is growth capex appropriately stress-tested for cost overruns and delivery into potentially weaker commodity markets?

### 3.2 Working Capital and Liquidity Demands

- **Moderate working capital intensity**: Revenue is collected relatively quickly in commodity markets. Inventory holdings (ore stockpiles, finished metal) create working capital requirements that fluctuate with commodity prices.
- **Concentrate and metal inventory**: Mining companies hold physical inventory of ore, concentrate, and refined metal. Rising commodity prices increase inventory value and working capital requirements.
- **Steel working capital**: Steel service center and end-user inventory cycles can create significant working capital swings. Scrap steel prices affect EAF/mini-mill working capital.
- **Hedging collateral**: Margin calls on commodity hedging positions can create unexpected liquidity demands during periods of commodity price volatility.

### 3.3 Asset Quality, Tangibility, and Liquidation Value

- **Hard assets with specialized use**: Mining assets are tangible but highly specialized. Value depends on commodity economics, ore body quality, and remaining reserve life. Location and infrastructure access affect transferability.
- **Mine life and reserve life**: Proved and probable reserves divided by annual production rate. Reserve life below 5 years without replacement projects signals depletion risk. Above 15 years provides long-term visibility. Quality of reserve estimates (measured, indicated, inferred) matters significantly.
- **Recovery value**: First-quartile assets in favorable jurisdictions attract buyers in distress. Fourth-quartile assets or those in high-risk jurisdictions may have limited recovery value. Environmental liabilities can exceed asset value for mature or depleted operations.
- **Steel assets**: Integrated mills have high replacement cost but may have limited value if not cost-competitive. EAF/mini-mills have lower replacement cost and more flexible siting.
- **Reclamation and closure liabilities**: Estimated cost of mine closure, environmental remediation, and land restoration. Underfunded reclamation obligations represent hidden liabilities that can impair recovery values.

### 3.4 Useful Life, Obsolescence, and Stranded Asset Risk

- **Mine life is finite**: Unlike manufacturing plants that can operate indefinitely with maintenance, mines deplete their ore bodies. Reserve life directly determines the remaining useful life of the asset.
- **Thermal coal stranded asset risk**: Thermal coal faces structural decline from the energy transition. ESG-driven financing restrictions have increased coal company cost of capital and reduced refinancing options. Banks and institutional investors restricting financing creates refinancing risk independent of operational performance.
- **Energy transition metals upside**: Battery metals (lithium, cobalt, nickel) and copper benefit from secular demand growth. EV production requires 4x more copper than ICE vehicles. Grid electrification and renewable energy installations drive additional copper and aluminum demand.
- **Battery chemistry evolution**: LFP vs NMC battery chemistry shifts demand between metals, creating technology risk for specific battery metal producers.
- **Aluminum lightweighting**: Secular demand driver from automotive fuel efficiency standards and packaging growth.

## Dimension 4: Structural Protection and Leverage Capacity

### 4.1 Leverage and Coverage Calibration

- Rating agencies assign sector-specific volatility factors that result in lower leverage tolerance for mining companies compared to less cyclical industries.
- A first-quartile producer with moderate leverage will receive a higher rating than a third-quartile producer with lower leverage, reflecting the survivability differential during price troughs.
- Cost curve position and asset quality are weighted above leverage metrics in rating assessments.
- Diversified miners with investment-grade ratings typically maintain Debt/EBITDA of 1.0-2.0x. Single-commodity HY miners face rating pressure above 3.0-4.0x due to extreme cash flow volatility.

### 4.2 Through-Cycle Normalization

- **S&P**: Uses normalized mid-cycle commodity price assumptions rather than current spot or forward prices for leverage calculations. Mid-cycle price decks are published annually and are deliberately conservative.
- **Moody's**: Applies commodity-specific price sensitivity analysis, stress-testing cash flow at multiple price scenarios (spot, forward curve, downside). Focuses on the sustainability of cash flow through a full commodity cycle and the company's ability to maintain adequate liquidity during prolonged downturns.
- **Normalization approach**: Use 5-10 year average commodity prices as mid-cycle reference. Adjust for structural shifts (e.g., trade tariffs providing permanent domestic price support, energy transition driving secular demand for copper). Leverage must be evaluated at trough pricing, not just current or mid-cycle.

### 4.3 Structural Features and Creditor Protections

- **Reserve-based covenants**: Some mining credit facilities include minimum reserve life requirements or reserve coverage ratios. Reserve restatements (due to price changes, grade adjustments, or methodology changes) can trigger covenant issues independent of operational performance.
- **Minimum production requirements**: Streaming and royalty agreements typically include minimum delivery requirements. Failure to meet minimums can trigger penalty payments or agreement termination. Credit agreements may include similar minimum throughput tests.
- **Hedging covenants**: Some lenders require minimum hedging levels (e.g., 25-50% of near-term production) to ensure cash flow predictability. Evaluate whether hedging covenants are achievable at current market prices and whether the hedging instruments are available in the required commodity.
- **Environmental compliance covenants**: Credit agreements may include representations regarding environmental permit compliance and require notification of material environmental incidents. Significant environmental liabilities can trigger technical defaults.
- **Reclamation bonding**: Mining permits require financial assurance for mine closure and remediation. Surety bonds, letters of credit, or cash deposits secure these obligations. Evaluate whether bonding capacity is adequate and whether bonding costs are increasing.
- **Royalty and streaming agreements**: Pre-existing royalty or streaming obligations take priority over unsecured creditors in many cases. Evaluate the terms, including precious metal delivery obligations, and assess the impact on free cash flow available for debt service.
- **Offtake agreements**: Contracted sales to smelters, traders, or end-users provide revenue certainty but may limit upside participation during price spikes. Evaluate contract terms, pricing mechanisms (spot, fixed, index-linked), and counterparty creditworthiness.
- **Capex limitations and cash sweep mechanisms**: Credit agreements may limit growth capex to preserve cash for debt service and include excess cash flow sweeps at elevated leverage levels. Cash sweeps triggered at commodity price thresholds ensure accelerated deleveraging during favorable pricing.

### 4.4 EBITDA Addback and Adjustment Quality

- **Exploration costs**: Evaluate whether exploration spending is excluded from adjusted EBITDA — for mining companies, some level of exploration is required to sustain the business.
- **Impairments and write-downs**: Reserve write-downs from lower commodity prices or grade adjustments may be excluded from adjusted EBITDA but represent real destruction of asset value.
- **Reclamation and closure accruals**: Evaluate whether these ongoing obligations are excluded from adjusted EBITDA calculations — they represent real future cash outflows.
- **Streaming and royalty costs**: These are real economic costs that may be treated differently depending on accounting classification (cost of revenue vs financing cost).
- **Foreign exchange gains/losses**: Currency movements can significantly affect reported EBITDA for miners with non-USD cost bases. Evaluate whether FX effects are normalized in adjusted EBITDA.

## Dimension 5: External and Systemic Risk

### 5.1 Regulatory and Legal Environment

- **Geopolitical and jurisdictional risk**: Mining assets are immovable and subject to host country regulatory, tax, and political risk. Resource nationalism (increased royalties, windfall taxes, export restrictions, nationalization threats) can impair economics. Jurisdictional quality varies dramatically: Australia and Canada are low-risk; DRC, Zambia, and certain Latin American countries carry elevated political risk.
- **Environmental regulation**: Tailings dam failures (Brumadinho, Samarco) have caused catastrophic environmental damage and billions in liabilities. Emissions regulations, water use restrictions, and biodiversity requirements increase operating costs. PFAS and acid mine drainage remediation create long-tail environmental obligations.
- **Mining permits and licensing**: Creating barriers to entry (credit positive for incumbents) but also operational risk if permits are threatened. Permitting timelines for new projects have lengthened significantly.
- **Trade policy**: Section 232 tariffs provide domestic price support but are subject to political change. Anti-dumping duties affect competitive dynamics and can be revised or removed.

### 5.2 Technology Disruption and Secular Displacement

- **Energy transition metals demand**: EV production requires 4x more copper than ICE vehicles. Battery chemistry evolution (LFP vs NMC) shifts demand between metals. Grid electrification and renewable energy installations drive additional copper and aluminum demand. This represents a major secular tailwind for select metals.
- **Thermal coal displacement**: Natural gas and renewables structurally displacing thermal coal in power generation. Metallurgical coal remains essential for steelmaking with limited substitution options currently, though hydrogen-based steelmaking (DRI) is emerging as a long-term alternative.
- **Automation and autonomous mining**: Autonomous haul trucks, remote-operated equipment, and digital mine optimization are reducing labor costs and improving safety but require significant upfront investment.
- **Recycling and secondary supply**: Nascent recycling industry for battery metals may eventually provide secondary supply that competes with primary production. Steel scrap recycling via EAF is already well-established.

### 5.3 Environmental, Climate, and ESG Exposure

- **ESG-driven financing restrictions**: Banks and institutional investors restricting coal financing have increased coal company cost of capital and reduced refinancing options. This trend is expanding to other high-emission mining activities.
- **Carbon pricing**: Carbon pricing and emissions regulations could impair higher-emission operations, particularly aluminum smelting (if coal-powered) and steel production.
- **Tailings and waste management**: Tailings dam failures represent catastrophic tail risk. Enhanced regulatory requirements and investor scrutiny following high-profile failures have increased compliance costs.
- **Water use**: Mining operations are significant water consumers. Water scarcity in key mining jurisdictions (Chile, Australia, parts of Africa) creates operational risk and community opposition.
- **Social license to operate**: Increasingly difficult to obtain for new mining projects. Community opposition, indigenous rights, and environmental impact assessments can delay or prevent project development.
- **PFAS and acid mine drainage**: Long-tail environmental obligations that create remediation costs extending well beyond mine closure.

### 5.4 Common Underwriting Traps and Red Flags

**Sector-specific red flags:**
- Cash costs above mid-cycle commodity prices for the primary commodity produced
- Mine life below 5 years without approved replacement projects or exploration success
- Single-asset or single-commodity concentration representing more than 70% of EBITDA
- Declining ore grades requiring increased processing throughput to maintain production
- Rising strip ratios (waste-to-ore) in open-pit operations increasing unit costs
- Sustaining capex increasing as a percentage of revenue without corresponding production growth (aging asset base)
- Environmental incidents (spills, tailings issues, water contamination) or escalating regulatory citations
- Host country jurisdiction deteriorating (new taxation, royalty increases, export restrictions, nationalization rhetoric)
- Hedging locked in at unfavorable prices relative to current market (underwater hedge book)
- Growing reclamation and closure liabilities without corresponding funding or bonding increases
- Growth capex materially exceeding initial project estimates without revised economic justification

**Typical underwriting traps:**
- Sizing leverage to peak commodity prices and treating current high prices as sustainable mid-cycle levels.
- Treating all mining credits with similar leverage tolerance regardless of cost curve position — a first-quartile producer at 3.0x is fundamentally different from a fourth-quartile producer at 3.0x.
- Underestimating construction and ramp-up risk for new mine development projects (30-50% cost overruns are common, not exceptional).
- Ignoring jurisdictional risk by focusing solely on asset quality and cost position without accounting for host country political, tax, and regulatory risk.
- Treating trade protection (Section 232) as permanent rather than as a regulatory advantage subject to policy reversal.

## Sub-Segments: Key Distinctions

- **Diversified miners (multi-commodity)**: The strongest credits in the sector due to commodity and geographic diversification. Revenue and cash flow from multiple metals reduces single-commodity sensitivity. These companies typically maintain investment-grade ratings with conservative financial policies. Scale advantages in procurement, shared infrastructure, and capital allocation flexibility.
- **Steel (integrated mills, EAF/mini-mills, specialty)**: Integrated mills (blast furnace) have higher fixed costs and greater sensitivity to iron ore and coking coal prices. EAF/mini-mills (Nucor, Steel Dynamics) use scrap steel feedstock, have lower fixed costs, and can flex production more rapidly. Specialty steel (stainless, alloy) commands premium pricing but faces smaller addressable markets. US steel benefits from Section 232 trade protection.
- **Aluminum**: Energy-intensive smelting creates natural advantage for producers with low-cost power (hydro). Alumina supply chain adds complexity. Aluminum demand benefits from lightweighting trends in automotive and packaging growth. Chinese overcapacity is a persistent pricing headwind.
- **Copper**: Secular demand growth from electrification and EV production. Supply constrained by declining grades at existing mines and lengthy permitting timelines for new projects. Copper miners typically maintain lower leverage than other metals sub-segments due to favorable long-term demand fundamentals.
- **Gold and precious metals**: Gold serves as an inflation hedge and safe-haven asset, providing some counter-cyclical demand characteristics. AISC is the standard profitability metric. Gold miners hedge less frequently than base metal producers. Exploration success and reserve replacement are critical for sustaining production.
- **Coal (metallurgical and thermal)**: Metallurgical coal remains essential for steelmaking with limited substitution options; thermal coal faces structural decline from the energy transition. ESG-driven financing restrictions have increased coal company cost of capital and reduced refinancing options. Met coal commands premium pricing and has stronger credit fundamentals than thermal coal.
- **Iron ore**: Dominated by four producers (Vale, Rio Tinto, BHP, Fortescue) controlling approximately 70% of seaborne supply. Chinese steel production drives demand. Quality differentials (iron content, impurities) create pricing tiers that favor higher-grade producers. Freight costs to China are a significant competitive factor.
- **Uranium**: Supply constrained by years of underinvestment following Fukushima. Nuclear power renaissance is driving renewed demand. Long-term contracting provides revenue visibility. Limited number of producing mines creates supply concentration risk.
- **Battery metals (lithium, cobalt, nickel)**: Fastest-growing sub-segment driven by EV battery demand. Lithium pricing has been extremely volatile. Battery chemistry evolution (LFP vs NMC) shifts demand between metals. Supply chains are geographically concentrated (DRC for cobalt, Australia/Chile for lithium), creating geopolitical risk. Nascent recycling industry may eventually provide secondary supply.

## Rating Agency Focus Areas

**S&P**: Uses normalized mid-cycle commodity price assumptions rather than current spot or forward prices for leverage calculations. Mid-cycle price decks are published annually and are deliberately conservative. Emphasizes cost curve position and asset quality as the primary determinants of business risk. Evaluates geographic and commodity diversification, penalizing single-asset and single-commodity companies with lower business risk assessments regardless of current profitability.

**Moody's**: Applies commodity-specific price sensitivity analysis, stress-testing cash flow at multiple price scenarios (spot, forward curve, downside). Focuses on the sustainability of cash flow through a full commodity cycle and the company's ability to maintain adequate liquidity during prolonged downturns. Evaluates financial policy discipline, particularly the willingness to reduce shareholder returns and growth capex during downturns. Assigns sector-specific volatility factors that result in lower leverage tolerance for mining companies compared to less cyclical industries.

Both agencies emphasize cost curve position and asset quality above all other factors. A first-quartile producer with moderate leverage will receive a higher rating than a third-quartile producer with lower leverage, reflecting the survivability differential during price troughs.
