## Unitranche Structure & First-Out / Last-Out Mechanics

### What a Unitranche Is

A unitranche is one borrower-facing facility that combines senior and subordinated economics into a single loan agreement. The borrower sees one instrument; the lenders allocate economics and control internally through an Agreement Among Lenders (AAL).

The underwriting question is not whether unitranche is simpler for the borrower. It is whether the internal FOLO structure changes control, recovery, or amendment incentives in a way that matters to the lender.

### Why Borrowers Use It

- Faster negotiation through one facility
- Cleaner borrower-facing documentation
- Greater certainty of committed capital
- Ability to combine senior and junior risk without separate intercreditor negotiations

Those advantages can be real, but they do not eliminate subordinated risk inside the capital stack. They compress it into a lender-to-lender agreement.

### Core FOLO Mechanics

#### Borrower-Facing Terms

The borrower typically sees:

- One principal amount
- One blended coupon
- One maturity and amortization schedule
- One covenant package
- One agent relationship

#### Lender-Facing Terms

The AAL determines:

- Payment priority between first-out and last-out
- Voting rights on amendments and waivers
- Who controls remedies in default
- Whether buy-out rights exist
- How fees, premiums, and recoveries are allocated

### AAL Areas to Read Closely

#### Payment Priority

Focus on:

- Scheduled interest and principal allocation
- Treatment of excess cash flow sweeps
- Default interest and fee allocation
- Handling of protective advances and agent expenses

#### Voting Rights

Focus on:

- Which matters require consent from both tranches
- Whether covenant waivers are first-out controlled
- Whether last-out can block structural changes
- Whether economics and collateral rights are aligned with voting power

#### Enforcement Control

Focus on:

- Who controls acceleration and collateral enforcement
- Whether there is an exclusive control period
- What happens after that period expires
- Whether buy-out rights create a realistic path to control transfer

### Underwriting Questions

1. Where does first-out stop and last-out begin in the capital structure?
2. Does the first-out tranche have enough control to behave like senior debt?
3. Is last-out being paid enough for subordinated loss absorption and delayed remedy control?
4. Can amendment or enforcement dynamics create conflict between the two lender classes?
5. Would the recovery path differ materially from a traditional first lien / second lien structure?

## Unitranche vs. Traditional Split Structures

| Dimension | Unitranche | Traditional Senior / Junior Split |
|---|---|---|
| Borrower-facing complexity | Lower | Higher |
| Intercreditor complexity | Hidden inside AAL | Explicit across separate documents |
| Amendment friction | One borrower document, plus lender coordination | Separate borrower and intercreditor coordination |
| Recovery analysis | Must read AAL carefully | Often more familiar lien and standstill structure |

Unitranche is simpler administratively, not structurally. The risk still exists; it is just packaged differently.

## Key Risks

### Last-Out Risk

- First dollar loss after first-out protection is exhausted
- Limited control during early enforcement
- Potential misalignment if first-out prioritizes speed over total value maximization

### First-Out Risk

- Relies on AAL drafting to preserve real seniority
- Can face amendment friction from last-out holders
- May receive weaker economics than standalone senior debt without equivalent structural certainty

### Shared Structural Risk

- Ambiguous AAL language around fees, sweeps, or remedy control
- Litigation risk when stress exposes drafting gaps
- Borrower exploiting differences in lender incentives during amendment negotiations

## Practical Output

When analyzing a unitranche, summarize:

1. Why the structure exists
2. Where first-out and last-out risk sit
3. Which AAL provisions matter most to control and recovery
4. Whether the blended economics compensate for the true risk transfer
