## Delayed Draw Term Loans, Accordions, and Commitment Structures

### What a DDTL Is

A delayed draw term loan is a committed but initially unfunded term facility that can be drawn during a defined availability period if agreed conditions are met.

The underwriting point is simple: a DDTL is contingent exposure, not optional upside. If the borrower has a real ability to draw it, the lender should understand how the credit looks when that capacity is used at the worst reasonable time.

## Core Mechanics

### DDTL

Use a DDTL when:

- Acquisition financing is expected but not immediate
- Capex or rollout needs are staged
- The lender wants committed future capital with pre-agreed conditions

The lender is obligated to fund if those conditions are satisfied.

### Accordion or Incremental Facility

An accordion is potential future capacity, but not a committed obligation. It typically requires fresh lender approval, pricing, and underwriting at the time it is added.

### Revolver

A revolver supports shorter-term liquidity and repeat borrowing rather than a one-way term draw. It should not be analyzed the same way as a DDTL even if both sit in the same facility package.

## Key Distinctions

| Feature | DDTL | Accordion | Revolver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commitment status | Committed at closing | Uncommitted until later approval | Committed revolving capacity |
| Draw purpose | Pre-identified future use | Future expansion capacity | Working capital and liquidity |
| Re-borrowing | No, once term-funded | N/A until created | Yes |
| Underwriting issue | Contingent leverage and timing | Future pricing and flexibility | Ongoing liquidity management |

## Draw Conditions to Review

The quality of a DDTL depends on what must be true at each draw. Focus on:

- No default or event of default
- Pro forma compliance with covenants
- Continued truth of key representations
- Use-of-proceeds limits
- Acquisition or capex-specific tests where relevant
- Evidence that the draw does not worsen a deteriorating credit without compensating control

Weak draw conditions can turn a DDTL into delayed underwriting.

## Modeling Implications

### Treat Undrawn Capacity as Real Risk

Model:

- Expected draw path in the base case
- No-draw case if business performs without the capital
- Full-draw downside case at the most fragile timing

### Stress the Right Variables

- Leverage after the draw
- Coverage after the draw
- Liquidity available to support integration or capex execution
- Whether covenants still provide meaningful headroom
- Whether the lender vehicle has enough capacity to honor the commitment

### Return Analysis

A DDTL can improve lender economics through commitment-related fees, but that benefit should not obscure the fact that the real underwriting may be the fully drawn exposure.

For current fee and pricing conventions, use `references/typical-deal-parameters.md`.

## Common Failure Modes

- Borrower can draw despite material business deterioration
- Acquisition criteria are too loose to protect the original underwrite
- Incremental or MFN provisions dilute the economics of the original facility
- Lender models only the closing leverage and ignores the contingent structure
- Fund or BDC liquidity is insufficient to fund commitments when multiple borrowers draw at once

## Practical Output

When analyzing a DDTL or accordion, summarize:

1. Why the contingent capital exists
2. What must happen before it can be used
3. How the credit looks if fully drawn
4. Whether the lender is being paid for the real contingent risk
