## Project Finance Fundamentals

Project finance funds a specific asset or concession through a ring-fenced borrower, typically an SPV, with repayment expected to come primarily from project cash flow and collateral rather than from broad recourse to the sponsor.

## What Makes It Different from Corporate Lending

Project finance is defined less by industry than by structure:

- repayment is tied to one project rather than a diversified enterprise
- lenders underwrite contracts, construction risk, and cash controls alongside economics
- collateral includes project assets, project accounts, and project contracts
- reserve accounts, step-in rights, and direct agreements do more work than they do in ordinary corporate lending

In corporate lending, poor performance can be offset by other business lines. In project finance, the project usually has to save itself.

## When the Structure Fits

Project finance fits best when:

- the asset is capital intensive and long lived
- cash flow can be allocated contractually and monitored directly
- risk can be assigned to parties best able to control it
- lenders can ring-fence the borrower and the cash waterfall

It fits poorly when repayment depends mainly on sponsor goodwill, weakly enforceable contracts, or assumptions about future refinancing that are not backed by current structure.

## Core Underwriting Questions

1. What contract or market mechanism creates the cash flow?
2. Which party bears construction, operating, market, and regulatory risk?
3. Can the lender control cash, contracts, and collateral after default?
4. Does the debt profile match the project's cash-generation and concession life?

## Project Structure and Key Parties

Project finance lives or dies by structure. This section maps the parties and contracts that determine who controls the project, who bears key risks, and what remains for the lender after stress.

## SPV

The SPV should be a ring-fenced borrower whose purpose is limited to owning, operating, and financing the project. At a minimum, the structure should support:

- separateness from sponsor insolvency
- ownership of the key assets and contracts
- controlled bank accounts
- transfer or enforcement mechanics that do not destroy value

## Core Contract Stack

### Concession or License

This document gives the project the right to exist. Review:

- contract term relative to debt life
- tariff or payment mechanics
- handback requirements
- change-in-law and force-majeure treatment
- termination compensation

### Offtake or Revenue Contract

This document gives the lender its repayment path. Review:

- whether payment depends on volume, availability, tolling, or merchant exposure
- pricing and indexation mechanics
- counterparty credit and replacement rights
- assignment and notice/cure rights for lenders

### EPC Contract

This document allocates construction risk. Review:

- scope certainty and change-order discipline
- time and performance guarantees
- remedies for delay or underperformance
- parent support, bonding, or other completion credit support

### O&M Agreement

This document allocates operating risk. Review:

- operator capability
- performance standards
- maintenance responsibilities
- consequences of underperformance or replacement

### Supply and Input Contracts

Where the project depends on fuel, feedstock, water, or other inputs, review:

- continuity of supply
- pricing pass-through or mismatch risk
- quality specifications
- term versus debt tenor

## Insurance and Direct Agreements

Insurance should preserve value after casualty rather than merely satisfy a documentary checklist. Direct agreements should preserve the key contracts through lender notice, cure, and step-in rights.

If the lender cannot keep the contracts alive after SPV distress, the security package may be weaker than it appears.

## Structural Questions

Use this reference to answer:

1. Which party controls the asset, the cash, and the contracts before and after default?
2. Which contract is most likely to fail first under stress?
3. Can the lender preserve project value long enough to cure, transfer, or sell the asset?
