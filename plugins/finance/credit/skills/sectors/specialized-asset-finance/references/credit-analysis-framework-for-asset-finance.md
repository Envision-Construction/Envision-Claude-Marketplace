## Credit Analysis Framework for Asset Finance

Asset finance requires two analyses at the same time:

1. **Collateral quality**: what is realistically recoverable if the borrower fails?
2. **Obligor quality**: how likely is the borrower to keep paying and preserve the asset?

The harder the asset is to liquidate or re-lease, the more the conclusion should depend on obligor credit rather than on appraised value alone.

## Asset Quality Lens

### Criticality

Ask whether the asset is mission-critical or optional. Borrowers fight harder to keep assets that directly support revenue or core operations.

### Liquidity

Ask whether the asset has a broad resale or leasing market. Standardized assets with transparent markets support stronger recovery assumptions than narrow-use or custom assets.

### Maintenance and Condition

Ask whether value depends on disciplined maintenance, regulatory compliance, or near-term overhaul spending. Deferred maintenance often erodes both operating performance and recovery value.

### Useful Life and Obsolescence

Ask whether debt maturity fits comfortably inside the asset's economic and regulatory life. Physical life is not enough if the asset can become commercially obsolete earlier.

## Obligor Lens

### Utilization and Revenue Conversion

Review whether the borrower can keep the asset productive. Falling utilization often appears before outright payment default.

### Balance Sheet Support

Review whether the borrower has enough liquidity and equity cushion to fund maintenance, bridge cyclical downturns, and absorb shocks without sacrificing the asset.

### Concentration

Single-asset or single-customer exposure can turn an otherwise acceptable structure into a binary credit.

### Operating Capability

Strong operators preserve asset value through maintenance, staffing, scheduling, and disciplined asset management. Weak operators can destroy value even when the end market remains acceptable.

## Bring the Lenses Together

Use the two lenses to categorize the deal:

- **Asset-led**: collateral remains defensible even if the borrower weakens
- **Balanced**: collateral and obligor matter roughly equally
- **Obligor-led**: asset value depends heavily on ongoing operations or a narrow user base

That categorization should influence leverage, covenant intensity, reporting, reserve design, and recovery expectations.

## Stress and Recovery Questions

Use downside analysis to answer:

1. What happens to collateral value if the borrower fails at the same time the market weakens?
2. How quickly can the lender take control, preserve condition, and monetize the asset?
3. Are liquidation costs, downtime, and remarketing delays large enough to change the true recovery case?
4. Does the structure remain defensible under both an operating stress and an enforcement stress?

## Diligence Checklist

Use this checklist as a durable lender reference. It is intentionally principles-based. Pull current thresholds, reserve norms, and stress calibration from the root references rather than embedding them here.

### Project Economics

- [ ] Identify what truly drives repayment: contracted revenue, regulated tariff, availability payment, charter or lease income, or residual realization.
- [ ] Test whether the revenue term, concession tail, or asset life extends beyond the debt tenor by a defensible margin.
- [ ] Review the main downside cases: volume, price, utilization, cost inflation, completion delay, and refinancing.
- [ ] Confirm that the model distinguishes base case, lender downside, and severe stress rather than blending them.

### Counterparties

- [ ] Review the credit strength and strategic importance of the offtaker, EPC contractor, operator, and major suppliers.
- [ ] Identify where replacement is realistic and where the lender is effectively locked into a single counterparty.
- [ ] Confirm whether guarantees, letters of credit, bonds, or reserve support backstop weak counterparties.

### Legal and Structural Protection

- [ ] Confirm that the SPV or borrower perimeter is narrow, separateness is preserved, and control rights are enforceable.
- [ ] Verify that direct agreements, account control, and assignment mechanics preserve lender value after default.
- [ ] Check that permits, concessions, and key licenses can survive distress, transfer, or lender step-in where needed.

### Construction and Operating Risk

- [ ] Determine whether construction risk is genuinely transferred or only described as transferred.
- [ ] Review maintenance responsibility, reserve philosophy, and the consequences of deferred upkeep.
- [ ] Confirm that the asset can continue operating safely and legally through the debt tenor and beyond any expected refinance.

### Financial Structure

- [ ] Tie coverage metrics to the actual cash waterfall and covenant definitions.
- [ ] Review leverage, reserve design, amortization shape, and any balloon or mini-perm dependency together rather than in isolation.
- [ ] Identify whether the structure relies on future refinancing, residual realization, or sponsor support to remain money-good.

## Post-Close Monitoring

### Regular Review Areas

- Recalculate DSCR, LLCR, and PLCR using the latest forecast and legal definitions.
- Review reserve account movements, especially repeated draws or delayed top-ups.
- Compare actual operating performance with the assumptions used to size debt.
- Reassess counterparty credit and contract performance, not just project-level cash flow.
- Track refinancing readiness well before any maturity or mini-perm reset date.

### Stress Testing Focus

- Revenue or utilization decline
- Cost inflation or operating disruption
- Rate and refinancing pressure
- Contract impairment or counterparty failure
- Residual-value erosion and exit-liquidity deterioration

## Red Flags

| Red Flag | Why It Matters | Lender Response |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage metrics trend down over multiple periods | Cushion is being consumed before a formal default occurs | Reforecast, tighten monitoring, and reassess distributions and sweep triggers |
| Reserve accounts are repeatedly drawn or underfunded | The structure is using future protection to solve current underperformance | Determine whether the issue is temporary timing or structural cash shortfall |
| Deferred maintenance or weak asset condition | Collateral value and operating reliability are both deteriorating | Require updated technical review and reassess residual assumptions |
| Counterparty payments or milestones slip | The lender may be underwriting a weakening contract, not just a project | Review cure rights, support package, and replacement options |
| Completion timetable drifts without clear accountability | Construction risk may be moving back to the borrower or lenders | Reconfirm contingency, support commitments, and independent engineer view |
| Refinancing becomes part of the base case rather than a contingency | The structure may be over-levered for hold-to-maturity support | Rework amortization, require more equity, or resize debt |
