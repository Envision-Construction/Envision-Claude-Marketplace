# Lease Analysis Deep Dive

Lease analysis determines whether current cash flow is durable, whether rollover creates upside or downside, and how much friction sits between today's occupancy and tomorrow's NOI.

## 1. Start with the Lease Profile

For each major tenant or lease cohort, identify:

- contractual rent
- expiry date and option structure
- lease type and reimbursement mechanics
- termination or contraction rights
- renewal economics
- tenant credit quality

The lease schedule should explain both cash flow concentration and timing risk.

## 2. Lease Structure Matters

| Lease Type | Main Credit Effect |
|---|---|
| **Triple net** | Shifts operating-cost risk away from the landlord, improving NOI visibility |
| **Modified gross** | Creates partial expense leakage and more judgment around recoveries |
| **Full service gross** | Makes the landlord more exposed to inflation and expense drift |
| **Percentage rent / turnover rent** | Links revenue to tenant sales and can introduce higher volatility |
| **Ground lease** | Usually creates durable income but concentrates risk in tenant credit and residual value |

Do not compare rents across lease types without adjusting for expense burden.

## 3. Mark-to-Market Analysis

The key question is not just whether rents are above or below market, but what happens when they reset.

### Evaluate:

- in-place rent versus current market rent
- effective rent after concessions, TI, and commissions
- renewal probability if rent is above market
- embedded upside if rent is below market
- whether market rent itself is sustainable

Above-market rent can make a current NOI look safer than it is. Below-market rent can create upside but should not be treated as automatic value creation.

## 4. Rollover and WALT

Short WALT is not automatically bad, and long WALT is not automatically safe.

| Lease Profile | Primary Concern |
|---|---|
| **Short WALT, below-market rent** | Execution risk, but possible upside |
| **Short WALT, above-market rent** | Near-term NOI reset risk |
| **Long WALT, weak tenant credit** | Income appears stable until a binary tenant event occurs |
| **Staggered rollover** | Usually healthier than concentrated expiration cliffs |

Always map the expiration schedule and identify years where revenue at risk is concentrated.

## 5. Renewal Probability

Renewal depends on more than rent.

### Higher renewal likelihood tends to come from:

- location-dependent business operations
- high tenant move cost or specialized buildout
- below-market rent
- long operating history in place
- limited competitive alternatives

### Lower renewal likelihood tends to come from:

- commoditized space with many substitutes
- above-market rent
- weak tenant credit or shrinking space need
- short decision windows and easy relocation

Use tenant-specific logic, not generic averages.

## 6. TI, LC, Downtime, and Concessions

Rollover risk is often a capital and timing problem before it becomes a rent problem.

Underwrite:

- tenant improvement needs
- leasing commissions
- free-rent or concession packages
- downtime between tenants
- operating drag during vacancy

If a building must spend heavily to defend occupancy, reported NOI overstates lender protection.

## 7. Special Clauses

Review leases for features that can break a normal underwriting model:

- early termination rights
- co-tenancy provisions
- go-dark rights
- contraction or expansion options
- unusual landlord obligations
- expense caps or exclusions

These clauses can shorten effective lease term or reduce the reliability of contractual rent.

## 8. Property-Type Differences

| Property Type | Lease Analysis Focus |
|---|---|
| **Office** | WALT, tenant credit, TI/LC burden, release downtime |
| **Retail** | Anchor role, co-tenancy, tenant sales, occupancy cost |
| **Industrial** | Functional utility, single-tenant dependence, release capex |
| **Multifamily** | Turnover, concessions, collections, affordability |
| **Hospitality** | Not lease-driven; use operating-contract and management analysis instead |

## 9. Output Template

A strong lease analysis should show:

1. **Tenant concentration** by rent and area
2. **Expiration schedule** and revenue at risk
3. **Mark-to-market view** for major tenants or unit cohorts
4. **TI/LC and downtime burden**
5. **Key lease clauses** that change effective term or economics
6. **Downside case** for major rollover or tenant loss

## Common Mistakes

- Looking only at WALT and not at who actually expires when
- Treating contractual rent as durable without checking market relevance
- Ignoring TI/LC, downtime, or concessions
- Comparing gross and net rents without normalizing expense burden
- Underwriting renewal by generic property-type average rather than tenant-specific logic
