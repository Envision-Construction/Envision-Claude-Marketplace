# Market and Submarket Analysis Framework

Market analysis should answer one question: **how durable are the subject asset's rents, occupancy, liquidity, and exit options relative to local supply and demand?**

Use `references/market-benchmarks.md` for current market metrics and `references/stress-scenario-framework.md` for current scenario sizes. This file explains how to interpret those inputs.

## 1. Start with the Correct Market Definition

Analyze the market at the level tenants and buyers actually choose from.

- **Market**: Broad metro or region that shapes capital flows and demand.
- **Submarket**: The local competitive area where rents, vacancy, and concessions are set.
- **Micro-location**: The immediate node, corridor, school district, logistics hub, or neighborhood that determines relative positioning.

If the subject competes in a narrower pocket than the reported broker submarket, underwrite to the narrower pocket.

## 2. Demand Drivers

| Demand Driver | What To Ask | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Employment / income base** | What supports tenant affordability or business occupancy demand? | Weak income support eventually pressures rents and collections |
| **Industry mix** | Is demand diversified or tied to one sector? | Concentration raises cyclical risk |
| **Population / household formation** | Is the user base growing, stable, or shrinking? | Especially relevant for housing, retail, and service uses |
| **Institutional anchors** | Are there durable employers, universities, hospitals, ports, or logistics nodes? | Creates depth and can stabilize occupancy |
| **Behavioral / use shift** | Has demand changed structurally for the product? | Office, retail, and specialized property types can re-rate structurally |

Do not confuse a recent tailwind with a durable demand base.

## 3. Supply and Competitive Pressure

Supply analysis is not just "how much is being built." It is whether new or shadow inventory can compete directly with the subject.

### Review

- Pipeline timing: when projects deliver relative to the subject's rollover or stabilization period
- Product overlap: whether new supply targets the same tenant or renter profile
- Barriers to entry: entitlement difficulty, land availability, capital intensity, and replacement economics
- Shadow supply: sublease space, dark boxes, lender-controlled inventory, or functionally obsolete space repriced into the market

### Key question

**If the subject loses a tenant or misses its lease-up plan, what competing space will it be forced to face?**

## 4. Vacancy, Absorption, and Concessions

Market tightness should be evaluated through multiple lenses, not one headline vacancy number.

| Lens | What To Check | Underwriting Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline vacancy** | Total marketed empty space | Broad market direction |
| **Economic vacancy** | Vacancy plus concessions and non-paying occupancy | Better indicator of effective pricing |
| **Net absorption** | Space or units taken minus vacated | Distinguishes healthy demand from temporary stability |
| **Concessions** | Free rent, TI, rent abatements, move-in incentives | Reveals softness before headline rents reprice |
| **Sublease / shadow inventory** | Competing unexpired space not captured cleanly in direct vacancy | Important in office and some industrial markets |

If asking rents appear stable while concessions rise, effective rent is weakening.

## 5. Rent Quality

The underwriting question is not "what is the asking rent?" It is **what rent can the subject actually achieve after concessions, downtime, and tenanting costs?**

### Test the rent story

- Compare in-place rent with market rent and recently signed comparable deals
- Distinguish asking rent from effective rent
- Assess whether recent rent growth came from demand, supply scarcity, amenity repositioning, or one-off volatility
- Check affordability or occupancy-cost constraints where tenant economics matter
- Identify whether the subject sits at the top, middle, or bottom of the local quality ladder

Rent growth unsupported by wages, tenant sales, or competitive positioning should be treated cautiously.

## 6. Competitive Set

Build a real comp set rather than a broker marketing set.

### Include assets that are:

- Similar in use and subtype
- Similar in quality and functional utility
- Located where the same user would realistically consider them
- Competing for the same price point or tenant profile

### Compare on:

- Occupancy
- Effective rent
- Concessions
- Quality and amenity package
- Access, visibility, or logistics advantage
- Recent leasing velocity

If better properties lease faster at higher rents, the subject may have a property-specific problem rather than a market problem.

## 7. Liquidity and Exit Depth

Markets with stable operations can still be weak collateral markets if buyer depth is poor.

Underwrite:

- Whether institutional, local, or user-buyer demand exists for the subject type
- Whether exit liquidity narrows sharply outside prime quality bands
- Whether the buyer universe disappears in stress or for specialized assets

Thin buyer depth means wider downside valuation ranges and slower disposition.

## 8. Scenario Framing

Translate market analysis into underwrite scenarios instead of leaving it as narrative.

| Scenario Type | Typical Trigger | What To Translate |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclical slowdown** | Slower demand and weaker absorption | Lower occupancy, slower release, softer rents |
| **Supply shock** | Heavy new deliveries or shadow inventory | Higher concessions, lower lease-up velocity |
| **Structural demand reset** | Product relevance weakens | Lower stabilized occupancy and wider exit cap |
| **Micro-location underperformance** | Subject loses competitive position | Higher tenanting cost and lower effective rent |

Use root stress references for magnitudes, then tailor the direction of the stress to the property and market.

## 9. Output Template

A good market section should conclude with:

1. **Market definition**: market, submarket, and subject competitive set
2. **Demand view**: what actually drives occupancy and rent
3. **Supply view**: what space is competing with the subject and when
4. **Subject position**: why the asset is better, average, or worse than peers
5. **Implication for underwriting**: rent, vacancy, concessions, lease-up, and exit liquidity assumptions

## Common Mistakes

- Using metro averages when the subject trades in a much narrower pocket
- Treating asking rents as achieved rents
- Ignoring concessions, sublease space, or shadow inventory
- Calling a market "strong" because of historical growth without checking current supply and affordability
- Treating all vacancy as equivalent when some is obsolete or noncompetitive
- Forgetting that weak exit liquidity is also a market risk
