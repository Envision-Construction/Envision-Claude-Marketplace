# Real Estate Alternative Credit Analysis

## Sector Overview

Alternative real estate here covers self-storage, cell towers and related digital-infrastructure sites, manufactured housing, and parking or mobility infrastructure. The same framework should work for a single asset, a portfolio, or a REIT because the core credit questions are demand durability, pricing power, concentration, capital intensity, and long-run relevance of the specific asset type. Some of these assets behave defensively with strong contracted or habitual demand; others are more exposed to local mobility patterns, tenant concentration, or technology shifts.

### Calibration lens: site control, demand density, and tenant stickiness

- **Short lease term does not always mean weak cash flow.** Self-storage can reprice rapidly and manufactured housing can be highly sticky even without long contractual tenor.
- **Long lease term does not eliminate concentration risk.** Towers can look highly contracted while still depending heavily on a few carriers, amend-and-extend economics, and underlying site-control terms.
- **Local scarcity matters more than headline asset category.** Parking, storage, and manufactured housing are all far more defensible when supply is constrained and local demand generators are durable.

### Cross-sector boundaries

- Use this file when the economics are primarily **real-estate site, rent, occupancy, utilization, and local-scarcity driven**.
- Use `real-estate-data-centers.md` when the key issues are **power availability, lease quality, customer concentration, and capex at a data-center property or portfolio**.
- Use `telecom.md` when the economics are primarily **subscriber, ARPU, network capex, or operating-company connectivity** driven rather than tower-site real estate.

## Subtype Matrix

| Subtype | Main demand driver | Key KPI | Main concentration risk | Main capex / asset risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-storage | Local moving activity, housing churn, and small-business demand | Occupancy and realized rate growth | Narrow market footprint or one operator-heavy market | Deferred maintenance, security, and expansion oversupply |
| Towers / digital-infrastructure sites | Carrier network densification and spectrum deployment | Churn, amendment revenue, and tenant concentration | Top-carrier exposure and site-control rollover | Ground-rent escalation, structural upgrades, and power/backhaul needs |
| Manufactured housing | Affordable housing shortage and resident stickiness | Occupancy, collections, and home sales velocity | One-region concentration or aging community profile | Infrastructure upkeep, utility systems, and resident-capital sensitivity |
| Parking / mobility | Office, airport, hospitality, event, and urban traffic demand | Utilization, transient mix, and rate realization | Dependence on one demand generator or district | Pavement/garage capex, structural repair, and mobility-pattern disruption |

## Dimension 1: Cash Flow Quality and Predictability

### 1.1 Revenue / Income Visibility
Visibility varies widely: towers usually benefit from long leases, low churn, and amendment revenue; self-storage from daily observable occupancy and fast repricing; manufactured housing from sticky tenancy and collections; and parking from local traffic, contract mix, and utilization patterns.

### 1.2 Cyclicality and Macro Sensitivity
The group is generally less cyclical than office or hotels, but each subtype has its own drivers, including wireless spending for towers, moving and housing affordability for storage and manufactured housing, and urban activity or commuter demand for parking.

### 1.3 Seasonality and Cash Flow Timing
Storage and parking can be seasonal, towers usually are not, and manufactured housing tends to be steadier but can still show occupancy and collection shifts tied to local employment and resident affordability.

### 1.4 Concentration Risk
Concentration often matters more than headline diversification suggests: towers can depend on a few carriers, parking on a few demand generators, storage on a few submarkets, and manufactured housing portfolios on a narrow geographic footprint or a limited set of communities.

## Dimension 2: Margin and Cost Structure Resilience

### 2.1 Pricing Power and Rate-Setting Ability
Pricing power comes from site criticality, switching cost, or local supply scarcity rather than broad daily market repricing, and it varies materially across the group. Self-storage and parking can reprice quickly, towers reprice through escalators and amendments, and manufactured housing depends more on local affordability and regulatory room to push rents.

### 2.2 Operating Leverage and Cost Flexibility
Most of these assets have favorable operating leverage once stabilized, though parking and certain mobility assets can still show sharper margin swings when utilization falls.

### 2.3 Input Cost and Inflation Exposure
Ground rent, taxes, insurance, utilities, site maintenance, security, and localized capital needs are the main costs; towers and parking can also face site-control and pavement or structural reinvestment needs.

### 2.4 Through-Cycle Margin Stability
Margins are generally stronger than for hotels or office, but they can still change materially when tenant concentration, regulatory shifts, site-control terms, or local demand drivers weaken.

## Dimension 3: Capital Requirements and Asset Profile

### 3.1 Capital Expenditure / Investment Intensity
Recurring capex is moderate but not trivial because paving, lighting, drainage, utility work, structural upgrades, and amenity or safety improvements all matter depending on subtype. Towers also require site and equipment reinforcement over time, while manufactured housing often faces lumpy utility and infrastructure work.

### 3.2 Working Capital and Liquidity Demands
Working capital is usually light, with liquidity pressure more likely to come from acquisition funding, site-control renewals, capital projects, or debt maturities than from ordinary operating swings.

### 3.3 Asset Quality, Tangibility, and Liquidation Value
The assets are tangible, but value depends heavily on location, legal rights, site control, tenant profile, and whether the specific asset still serves a scarce use case in its market.

### 3.4 Useful Life, Obsolescence, and Stranded Asset Risk
Long asset life is usually a strength, but obsolescence can still emerge if technology, mobility patterns, zoning, or local demand shift against the current use.

## Dimension 4: Structural Protection and Leverage Capacity

### 4.1 Leverage and Coverage Calibration
Leverage can often be higher than in more cyclical property types where revenue is sticky or contracted, but concentration, site-control, and subtype-specific demand risk should still cap tolerance.

### 4.2 Through-Cycle Normalization
Normalize occupancy, utilization, lease renewal, rent escalators, churn, and subtype-specific capex rather than annualizing unusually strong local conditions. Storage supply pipelines, tower amendment pace, manufactured-housing rent growth, and parking transient demand should all be normalized separately.

### 4.3 Structural Features and Creditor Protections
Ground-lease tenor, reserve structures, recourse, capex funding, and unsecured-versus-secured flexibility matter in both single-asset and portfolio or REIT structures. For towers especially, underlying site-control duration should be compared directly with tenant lease life and debt maturity.

### 4.4 EBITDA Addback and Adjustment Quality
Question assumptions that future amendments, utilization gains, or local demand normalization will arrive without friction, and scrutinize any capex-light stabilization narratives closely.

## Dimension 5: External and Systemic Risk

### 5.1 Regulatory and Legal Environment
Permitting, zoning, tenant protections, utility access, local mobility policy, and tax regimes can all materially affect economics and exit liquidity.

### 5.2 Technology Disruption and Secular Displacement
Technology can be a core differentiator here: towers face network-architecture evolution, parking faces mobility and autonomy shifts, and even storage and manufactured housing can be affected by local land-use and digital operating changes.

### 5.3 Environmental, Climate, and ESG Exposure
Storm resilience, flood or fire exposure, site safety, insurability, and governance around resident or tenant treatment are the main ESG lenses across the group.

### 5.4 Common Underwriting Traps and Sector-Specific Red Flags
Common mistakes include ignoring tenant concentration, assuming long-lived assets cannot become obsolete, underestimating site-control rollover, and giving full credit to speculative future amendments, utilization gains, or redevelopment potential.

## Key Metrics / Watchlist

- **Self-storage**: Occupancy trend, realized rent change, move-in versus move-out spread, new-supply pipeline, and revenue per occupied square foot.
- **Towers**: Top-carrier exposure, churn, amendment revenue mix, remaining site-control term, and ground-rent escalation profile.
- **Manufactured housing**: Occupancy, collections, resident turnover, rent-to-income sensitivity, and community-infrastructure capex.
- **Parking**: Utilization, transient versus contract mix, rate realization, exposure to office or airport demand, and deferred structural maintenance.
- **Across the group**: Geographic concentration, recurring maintenance capex, insurance cost, refinancing dependence, and local policy or zoning change risk.

## Handoff to Credit Modeling

For `modeling-and-valuation`, stress occupancy or utilization, renewal pricing, churn, site-control or ground-rent escalation, recurring maintenance capex, and refinance terms together, with subtype-specific downside for towers, storage, manufactured housing, or parking economics.

- For self-storage, combine occupancy pressure with slower realized-rate growth and a local supply response.
- For towers, stress churn, weaker amendment activity, and site-control mismatch against debt maturity.
- For manufactured housing, test collections, rent-growth limits, and infrastructure capex together.
- For parking, link utilization downside directly to office, airport, hotel, or event demand assumptions rather than using a generic occupancy shock.
