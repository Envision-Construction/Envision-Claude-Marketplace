## Modern Restructuring Pathways

Distressed borrowers usually move along a spectrum from least invasive to most court-driven solutions. The correct path is determined by liquidity pressure, stakeholder alignment, documentary flexibility, and the amount of operational restructuring required.

### 1. Amend and Extend

- Best fit when the business remains fundamentally viable and the problem is mostly timing
- Changes often focus on maturity extension, covenant relief, or modest economics
- Usually preserves enterprise value if creditors believe the business can stabilize

### 2. Exchange Offer or Out-of-Court Restructuring

- Best fit when creditors are willing to trade economics for time or improved downside
- Works only if enough stakeholders can be aligned without court power
- Main risk is the holdout problem: non-participants may keep stronger claims while others compromise

### 3. Liability Management Exercise

- Best fit when the borrower has documentary flexibility and wants to reshuffle value without a formal filing
- Often benefits participating creditors and harms those left behind
- Requires close review of covenant language, sacred rights, and transfer mechanics

### 4. Restructuring Support Agreement

- Best fit when major constituencies can agree on core economics before filing
- Improves certainty and usually narrows the range of outcomes
- The key analytical question is who signed, who did not, and what leverage the non-signers retain

### 5. Prepackaged or Pre-Arranged Chapter 11

- Best fit when court tools are needed, but much of the economics can be negotiated in advance
- Preserves more value than a chaotic filing if creditor alignment is reasonably strong
- Useful when contract rejection, stay protection, or cramdown authority is necessary

### 6. Freefall Chapter 11

- Best fit only when the company lacks time or stakeholder alignment for a negotiated path
- Usually carries the widest recovery range because value, control, and timing remain in flux
- Requires heavier emphasis on DIP terms, litigation risk, and business erosion during the case

## Choosing the Path

Use four questions:

1. Is the problem mostly maturity/liquidity, or does the business model also need repair?
2. Can enough creditors be aligned without court power?
3. Does the company need legal tools that only Chapter 11 provides?
4. Is documentation flexible enough to enable an LME or exchange outside court?

## Common Mistakes

- Treating every distressed case as a bankruptcy case when the real issue is maturity management
- Treating every out-of-court path as consensual when documentation can shift value coercively
- Assuming a pre-negotiated filing eliminates downside; it narrows, but does not remove, path risk
- Ignoring the operational cost of delay when comparing negotiated and court-driven options

### Out-of-Court Restructuring Options

Out-of-court restructuring is an effort to solve a capital structure problem without using Chapter 11. It works best when stakeholders are sufficiently aligned and the company does not need court-only tools such as contract rejection, broad stay protection, or cramdown authority.

#### Why Companies Prefer It

- Avoid bankruptcy stigma and business disruption
- Preserve customer, vendor, and employee confidence
- Reduce process cost and management distraction
- Keep negotiations more private and flexible

#### Core Forms

**Consensual amendment**
- Extends maturities, loosens covenants, or adjusts economics
- Best when the business remains viable and creditors mainly need to buy time

**Exchange offer**
- Replaces old debt with new debt or mixed consideration on revised terms
- Best when creditors will accept some compromise in exchange for runway or improved downside

**Negotiated broader restructuring**
- Combines debt reduction, maturity extension, security enhancement, or sponsor support
- Best when key creditor groups can coordinate without formal court process

#### Main Constraint: The Holdout Problem

- Non-participating creditors may keep stronger claims while others compromise
- The more dispersed the creditor base, the harder pure consent becomes
- When holdouts have blocking leverage, an out-of-court path can fail even if the economics are sensible

#### Questions to Ask

1. Does the company need legal tools only available in Chapter 11?
2. Can enough creditors be aligned voluntarily?
3. Is the value transfer among creditor groups acceptable without court enforcement?
4. Does the company have enough liquidity to negotiate without filing?

#### Common Mistakes

- Assuming out-of-court always means consensual and low-risk
- Ignoring the leverage of non-participating creditors
- Treating maturity extension as a fix when the business model still does not work
- Overlooking whether documentation permits coercive tactics outside court
