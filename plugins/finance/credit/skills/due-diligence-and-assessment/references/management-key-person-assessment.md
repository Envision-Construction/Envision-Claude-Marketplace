# Management & Key Person Assessment

Integrated framework for evaluating management teams and key person risk from a credit perspective, with particular focus on leveraged and middle-market companies.

---

## Part 1: Management Evaluation Framework

### Credit vs Equity Perspective on Management

Management evaluation for credit differs fundamentally from the equity investor's lens. Equity investors reward risk-taking, growth acceleration, and market share capture. Credit analysts value balance sheet discipline, operational execution consistency, capital allocation conservatism, and transparent communication. A CEO celebrated by equity markets for bold M&A may be a red flag for lenders if those acquisitions are funded with excessive leverage.

The credit analyst asks: will this management team protect my downside? Will they prioritize debt service over growth ambitions when conditions deteriorate?

### Key Assessment Areas

#### Track Record Through Cycles
- How did management perform during the last period of stress relevant to this business or sector?
- Did they proactively cut costs or wait until forced by covenant pressure?
- History of meeting or exceeding financial guidance; repeated misses are a meaningful negative signal
- Capital allocation decisions during stress: did they preserve liquidity or pursue opportunistic growth?
- Relevant experience managing businesses of comparable scale and complexity

#### Industry Experience and Operational Depth
- Years of sector-specific experience for CEO, CFO, and COO
- Breadth of management team — concentrated key-person dependency is a material concern in smaller or less institutionalized credits
- Bench strength and internal promotion track record
- Relationships with key customers, suppliers, and regulators
- Technical expertise appropriate to the business (e.g., a healthcare services CEO should understand reimbursement dynamics, not just financial engineering)

#### Succession Planning
- Identified successors for top 3-5 positions
- Age and tenure profile of senior team — a team averaging 60+ years with no clear successors is a risk
- History of orderly transitions vs disruptive departures
- Depth of middle management as a talent pipeline
- Contractual protections (non-competes, retention packages)

#### Financial Transparency and Communication Quality
- Earnings call tone: does management acknowledge challenges or consistently deflect?
- Quality of financial disclosures — detailed segment reporting, clear EBITDA reconciliations, transparent addback explanations
- Responsiveness to lender/investor inquiries
- Consistency between public statements and private lender presentations
- Willingness to provide monthly financials or enhanced reporting during stress periods

#### Capital Allocation Philosophy
- Historical leverage targets and actual leverage trajectory
- Dividend/distribution policy relative to free cash flow generation
- M&A discipline: purchase price multiples paid, integration track record, write-down history
- Share repurchase behavior (for public companies) — aggressive buybacks funded with debt are a negative signal
- Capex discipline: maintenance vs growth, ROI tracking, history of cost overruns

### Red Flags

#### Excessive Management Turnover
- CFO departures are the most significant red flag — two CFO changes in three years demands deep investigation
- Turnover concentrated in finance/accounting roles may signal reporting issues
- Glassdoor reviews citing "toxic culture" or "leadership instability" should not be dismissed
- Controller-level turnover can indicate pressure to produce aggressive numbers

#### Aggressive Accounting Practices
- Revenue recognition changes that boost short-term results
- Capitalization of costs that peers expense (e.g., software development, customer acquisition costs)
- EBITDA addbacks exceeding 20% of reported EBITDA on a sustained basis
- Frequent restatements or material weakness disclosures
- Auditor changes, especially away from Big 4 to regional firms

#### Related-Party Transactions
- Management consulting fees paid to sponsor affiliates
- Leases with entities controlled by management or sponsors
- Procurement through affiliated vendors without competitive bidding
- Management company fees that escalate regardless of performance
- Any transaction where the counterparty is a family member or personal associate of an officer

#### Compensation Misalignment
- Executive compensation tied primarily to revenue growth or EBITDA (without leverage-adjusted metrics)
- Golden parachutes that incentivize a sale regardless of credit impact
- Stock options with strike prices far below current trading levels (all upside, no downside)
- Bonus structures based on adjusted metrics that exclude items management controls
- Compensation levels grossly disproportionate to company size or complexity

### Evaluating Management Without Direct Access

Most credit analysts do not get face-to-face management meetings. Practical sources include:

- **SEC Filings**: Proxy statements (DEF 14A) for compensation, related-party transactions, board composition. 10-K risk factors and MD&A for management tone and disclosure quality.
- **Earnings Calls**: Tone, responsiveness to tough questions, consistency of messaging over time. Transcript archives allow tracking how management described the business 2-3 years ago vs reality.
- **Industry Reputation**: Trade publications, conference appearances, peer commentary. Industry contacts can provide invaluable off-the-record assessments.
- **Employee Reviews**: Glassdoor, Blind, LinkedIn attrition patterns. High turnover in finance and operations roles is a leading indicator.
- **Legal Databases**: PACER for litigation involving officers, SEC enforcement actions, regulatory orders.

### Management Quality vs Business Quality

Strong management in a structurally declining business can slow deterioration but rarely reverse it. A skilled operator can extend the runway — optimize costs, manage working capital, find niche opportunities — but cannot overcome secular headwinds like technological displacement or regulatory obsolescence.

Conversely, a mediocre management team in a strong, growing business can coast for years on the business's inherent momentum. The business quality provides a margin of safety that compensates for suboptimal execution.

**The critical question for credit**: is the leverage appropriate given BOTH the business quality and the management quality? Higher-leverage credits require either a stronger business, stronger management, or both; weaker management reduces the margin for error quickly.

When management quality is the primary investment thesis (i.e., "we're lending because this team is excellent"), the analyst should stress-test what happens if key personnel depart. If the credit thesis collapses without specific individuals, the risk is not adequately compensated at typical leveraged finance spreads.

## Part 2: Key Person Risk Assessment

### Key Person Identification

#### Systematic Identification Process

Evaluate each senior executive against three criteria to determine key person status:

| Criterion | Assessment | Key Person if... |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue Dependency | How much revenue is tied to this individual's relationships? | >15% of revenue depends on their client relationships |
| Operational Capability | What institutional knowledge or technical expertise would be lost? | Critical processes exist only in this person's knowledge |
| Replacement Difficulty | How long to find and onboard a qualified replacement? | >6 months to recruit and >12 months to be fully effective |

#### Common Key Person Roles

| Role | Typical Key Person Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|
| CEO / Founder | HIGH — especially in founder-led companies | Vision, culture, external relationships, brand association |
| CFO | MEDIUM-HIGH | Financial reporting integrity, banking relationships, investor communication |
| Head of Sales / CRO | HIGH — in relationship-driven businesses | Client relationships, revenue pipeline, sales team leadership |
| CTO / Chief Scientist | HIGH — in technology/biotech companies | Technical architecture decisions, IP knowledge, R&D direction |
| Key Client Relationship Managers | MEDIUM-HIGH | Direct revenue attachment, client trust, contract renewal influence |
| Chief Medical Officer | HIGH — in healthcare/pharma | Regulatory relationships, clinical development expertise |

### Departure Impact Modeling

#### Revenue at Risk Assessment

| Factor | Lower Revenue Risk | Higher Revenue Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Client Relationships | Institutional relationships; multiple touchpoints | Personal relationships; single contact |
| Contract Structure | Long-term contracts with the company | Verbal agreements tied to individual |
| Non-Compete/Non-Solicit | Strong protections with adequate scope | No protections or unenforceable clauses |
| Client Diversification | Revenue spread across many clients | Revenue concentrated in relationships managed by key person |
| Transition Period | 6+ month transition possible | Abrupt departure likely |

#### Quantification Framework

For each identified key person, estimate:

| Impact Dimension | Assessment | Risk Score (1-5) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue at Risk | % of revenue directly dependent on individual | |
| Operational Disruption | Duration and severity of operational impact from departure | |
| Replacement Timeline | Time to hire + time to effectiveness | |
| Knowledge Loss | Undocumented processes, relationships, institutional knowledge | |
| Team Impact | Risk of follow-on departures (direct reports, loyal team members) | |
| **Composite Key Person Risk** | **Weighted Average** | |

#### Scenario Modeling

| Scenario | Assumptions | Credit Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Orderly Transition | 6-month notice; successor identified; overlap period | Revenue decline 5-10%; recovers within 2-3 quarters |
| Unplanned Departure | Immediate departure; no successor ready | Revenue decline 10-20%; 3-6 quarter recovery |
| Departure to Competitor | Key person joins competitor; solicits clients | Revenue decline 15-30%; partial client loss may be permanent |
| Health/Incapacity | Sudden absence; no immediate resolution | Similar to unplanned departure + uncertainty premium |

### Succession Planning Assessment

#### Evaluation Criteria

| Element | Strong Succession Planning | Weak Succession Planning |
|---|---|---|
| Documented Plan | Written succession plan reviewed annually by board | No documented plan |
| Internal Candidates | 2+ identified successors for each critical role | No identified successors |
| Development Programs | Active leadership development and mentoring programs | No formal development |
| Cross-Training | Multiple executives capable of covering critical functions | Single point of failure for key functions |
| Emergency Succession | Board-approved emergency succession for CEO/CFO | No emergency plan |
| External Pipeline | Ongoing relationship with executive recruiters | No external relationships |

#### Succession Readiness Assessment

| Readiness Level | Description | Risk Level |
|---|---|---|
| Ready Now | Internal candidate identified and capable of assuming role immediately | LOW |
| Ready in 1-2 Years | Internal candidate with development plan underway | LOW-MEDIUM |
| External Hire Needed | No internal candidate; must recruit externally | MEDIUM-HIGH |
| No Plan | No succession planning; no candidates identified | HIGH |

### Retention Mechanisms

#### Common Retention Tools

| Mechanism | Typical Structure | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Equity/Options (vesting) | 4-5 year vesting schedule; cliff + ratable | HIGH — significant economic incentive to stay |
| Deferred Compensation | Cash retention bonuses with 2-3 year deferred payout | MEDIUM-HIGH — creates economic penalty for departure |
| Non-Compete/Non-Solicit | 12-24 month restriction on competing or soliciting clients/employees | MEDIUM — enforceability varies by state |
| Long-Term Incentive Plan (LTIP) | Cash or equity awards tied to 3-5 year performance | HIGH — aligns retention with performance |
| Golden Handcuffs | Forfeiture of unvested equity/deferred comp upon voluntary departure | HIGH — economic lock-in |
| Phantom Equity / SAR | Synthetic equity participation without ownership dilution | MEDIUM-HIGH — common in private credit deals |

#### Retention Adequacy Assessment

| Factor | Adequate | Inadequate |
|---|---|---|
| Unvested Compensation | >1x annual compensation unvested at any given time | Minimal unvested compensation; most already vested |
| Non-Compete Scope | Geographic and temporal scope appropriate for business | Overly narrow or in non-enforcement state (California) |
| Performance Vesting | Achievable targets tied to EBITDA/FCF metrics | Unrealistic targets that eliminate retention value |
| Change of Control | Accelerated vesting protects in acquisition scenario | No protection; uncertainty upon change of control |

### Key Person Clauses in Credit Agreements

#### Types of Key Person Provisions

| Provision | Trigger | Consequence | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reporting Requirement | Departure of CEO, CFO, or named key persons | Borrower must notify agent within specified period | COMMON |
| Replacement Requirement | Key person departure | Must hire replacement within 90-180 days | MODERATE |
| Consultation Right | Key person departure | Lender has right to meet with borrower to discuss plan | MODERATE |
| Event of Default | Key person departure without timely replacement | Technical default (rare) | RARE |
| Mandatory Prepayment | Key person departure triggers partial prepayment | Cash sweep or partial prepayment required | VERY RARE |

#### Assessment of Clause Adequacy

| Question | If Yes - Adequate | If No - Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Does the credit agreement name specific key persons? | Targeted protection | Generic language may be too broad or too narrow |
| Is the replacement timeline reasonable (90-180 days)? | Allows proper search | Too short (<60 days) may be unrealistic |
| Are consequences meaningful but proportionate? | Appropriate protection | No consequence = no value; default trigger = disproportionate |

### Size-Adjusted Risk Assessment

#### Key Person Risk by Company Size

| Company Size (EBITDA) | Typical Key Person Risk | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| >$200M | LOW-MEDIUM | Deep management bench; institutional processes |
| $100M-$200M | MEDIUM | Functional leaders important but replaceable |
| $50M-$100M | MEDIUM-HIGH | CEO/CFO often critical; limited bench depth |
| $25M-$50M | HIGH | Founder/CEO frequently irreplaceable; flat organization |
| <$25M | VERY HIGH | Company may be synonymous with 1-2 individuals |

#### Middle Market Considerations

For companies with <$100M EBITDA (common in private credit):
- **Request detailed organizational charts** during due diligence
- **Interview key persons** to assess their commitment and timeline
- **Assess family/personal factors** that may affect retention (relocation, retirement age, health)
- **Evaluate the management team as a whole**, not just the CEO — if the management team was installed by the sponsor 12 months ago, assess whether they have had adequate time to learn the business

### PE-Backed Company Assessment

#### Sponsor-Installed Management

| Assessment Area | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Management Tenure | >18 months in role; established relationships | <12 months; still learning the business |
| Prior Industry Experience | Multiple years in same or adjacent industry | First-time in the sector |
| Sponsor Relationship | Worked with sponsor on prior deals (track record) | First deal with this sponsor |
| Team Cohesion | Management team worked together before | Assembled from different backgrounds; no prior collaboration |
| Board Support | Active sponsor support and resources | Sponsor disengaged or distracted by other portfolio issues |

#### Post-LBO Key Person Dynamics

| Timeframe | Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Year 0-1 (post-close) | Management adjusting to new capital structure and reporting requirements | Active sponsor oversight; additional resources |
| Year 1-3 | Key pre-acquisition managers may depart after retention bonuses vest | Staggered vesting; new LTIP for next phase |
| Year 3-5 | Management fatigue; equity value uncertain | Refresh incentive plan; reassess MOIC projections |
| Year 5+ (extended hold) | Management may seek exit; sponsor attention waning | Address proactively; consider management buyout option |

### Key Person Red Flags Checklist

- [ ] CEO/founder with no documented succession plan
- [ ] CFO departure within 12 months of LBO closing (concerning given CFO role in integration)
- [ ] Multiple C-suite departures in rolling 12-month period (per due-diligence handoff rule: >2 C-suite changes triggers enhanced documentation review)
- [ ] Key person's non-compete expired or in non-enforcement jurisdiction
- [ ] Revenue concentration >15% tied to a single individual's client relationships
- [ ] Management team tenure <12 months in a recently acquired company
- [ ] No equity incentive or deferred compensation for critical executives
- [ ] Key technical leader with undocumented proprietary knowledge
- [ ] Founder approaching retirement age with no transition plan
- [ ] Key person recently received outside job offer or made public comments about burnout/transition

## Part 3: Management Assessment Quick Checklist

### Executive Team Profile
- [ ] Identify CEO, CFO, COO, and other key C-suite members
- [ ] Document tenure in current role and with the company
- [ ] Review prior roles and industry experience (LinkedIn, SEC filings)
- [ ] Assess key-person risk — would departure of any single executive materially impair operations?
- [ ] Review employment agreements, non-competes, and retention arrangements (proxy statement)

### Track Record Evaluation
- [ ] Analyze financial performance under current management (3-5 years minimum)
- [ ] Compare management guidance to actual results — track hit/miss pattern
- [ ] Review performance through last economic downturn or industry disruption
- [ ] Assess M&A track record — purchase multiples, integration success, write-down history
- [ ] Evaluate organic growth vs acquisition-driven growth under current management

### Governance and Compensation
- [ ] Review board composition — independence, expertise, conflicts of interest
- [ ] Analyze executive compensation structure (base, bonus, equity, incentives)
- [ ] Identify compensation metrics — are they aligned with credit outcomes (leverage, FCF) or only equity outcomes (EBITDA, revenue)?
- [ ] Screen for related-party transactions (proxy statement, 10-K footnotes)
- [ ] Review management fee arrangements with sponsor (if PE-backed)

### Financial Transparency
- [ ] Assess quality of financial disclosures and earnings call commentary
- [ ] Review audit opinions — clean, qualified, material weakness disclosures
- [ ] Check for auditor changes in the past 3 years and reasons given
- [ ] Evaluate EBITDA addback reasonableness and consistency
- [ ] Review history of financial restatements

### Qualitative Signals
- [ ] Read last 4-8 earnings call transcripts for tone and responsiveness
- [ ] Review Glassdoor ratings and employee sentiment (focus on finance and operations roles)
- [ ] Check PACER/SEC for litigation or enforcement involving officers
- [ ] Assess industry reputation through trade publications and contacts
- [ ] Review LinkedIn for unusual patterns of departures from the finance team

### Key Data Sources

| Source | Information Available |
|--------|---------------------|
| SEC EDGAR (10-K, 10-Q, DEF 14A) | Financials, compensation, governance, related-party transactions |
| Earnings call transcripts | Management tone, guidance accuracy, strategic direction |
| Proxy statements (DEF 14A) | Board composition, executive comp, shareholder proposals |
| LinkedIn | Management backgrounds, tenure, team stability |
| Glassdoor / Blind | Employee sentiment, management ratings, cultural issues |
| PACER | Federal litigation involving officers or the company |
| Rating agency reports | Credit opinions on management quality |
