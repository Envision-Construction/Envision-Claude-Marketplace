# Red Flags by Document Type

## Financial Statements

### Critical Red Flags
- **Qualified audit opinion**: Any qualification other than a standard clean opinion requires investigation. A going concern qualification is the most severe — it signals the auditor questions the company's ability to continue operating.
- **Frequent restatements**: More than one restatement in a 3-year period indicates systemic issues with financial reporting quality. Investigate the nature and magnitude of each restatement.
- **Auditor change**: A change from a Big 4 firm to a regional or local firm may signal the previous auditor declined to continue the engagement. This is particularly concerning if the change coincides with aggressive accounting practices.
- **Material weakness in internal controls**: Disclosed in the 10-K or audit report. Material weaknesses in revenue recognition, accounts receivable, or inventory are the most concerning for credit analysis.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Aggressive revenue recognition**: Revenue growing significantly faster than cash collection (AR growing faster than revenue). Percentage-of-completion accounting with material estimates. Bill-and-hold arrangements. Channel stuffing indicators (Q4 revenue spikes with Q1 returns).
- **Unusual related-party transactions**: Revenue from or payments to entities controlled by management, officers, or significant shareholders. Lease arrangements with related parties at above-market rates. Loans to or from officers or directors.
- **Growing gap between GAAP and non-GAAP results**: A widening spread between reported earnings and management's "adjusted" figures suggests increasing reliance on adjustments rather than genuine operational improvement.
- **Off-balance-sheet obligations**: Variable interest entities, operating lease obligations (pre-ASC 842), purchase commitments, factoring arrangements, or synthetic leases that create unrecognized liabilities.
- **Inconsistent accounting policies**: Changes in depreciation methods, capitalization thresholds, or inventory methods that systematically improve reported results. Each change may be defensible individually, but a pattern suggests earnings management.

### Yellow Flags
- **Declining cash conversion cycle**: If DSO is lengthening, DIO is increasing, or DPO is being stretched significantly, the company may be managing working capital aggressively to show better cash flow.
- **Capex below depreciation for extended periods**: Suggests underinvestment in the asset base — future capex may need to catch up, creating a cash flow headwind.
- **Significant goodwill relative to total assets**: High goodwill (>40% of total assets) indicates historical acquisitions at premium prices. Goodwill impairment risk is elevated if business performance deteriorates.

## Quality of Earnings (QoE) Report

### Critical Red Flags
- **Total EBITDA adjustments exceeding 25% of reported EBITDA**: The adjusted number is so far from the reported number that the company's true earning power is uncertain.
- **Recurring "non-recurring" items**: If the same type of adjustment appears in 2 or more of the 3 trailing years, it is by definition not non-recurring. Common examples: restructuring charges, acquisition-related costs, litigation settlements, severance. A company with $5M of "non-recurring" restructuring charges every year for 5 years has a recurring $5M expense.
- **Synergy or run-rate adjustments without clear support**: Projected cost savings that have not been implemented, revenue synergies from cross-selling that has not been tested, or "run-rate" revenue from contracts that are not yet signed.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Significant working capital adjustments**: Large adjustments to normalize working capital may indicate seasonal distortion, aggressive collection practices pre-sale, or payable stretching to improve cash position for due diligence.
- **Management projections detached from historical performance**: If the company has grown revenue 3-5% annually for 5 years and the projection shows 10-15% growth, the disconnect requires a convincing explanation.
- **QoE provider hedging language**: Excessive use of qualifiers ("we were informed by management," "we have not independently verified," "assuming management's representations are accurate") signals the QoE provider is not standing behind the numbers.
- **Missing or incomplete analysis sections**: If the QoE skips working capital normalization, does not address customer concentration, or omits capex analysis, the report may be incomplete by design.

### Yellow Flags
- **Customer concentration increasing**: Top 5 customer concentration growing from 25% to 40% over 3 years indicates increasing risk even if total revenue is growing.
- **Revenue quality shifting**: Shift from recurring/contractual revenue to project-based or transactional revenue reduces predictability.
- **Pro forma adjustments for recent acquisitions**: Annualizing revenue from a business acquired 3 months ago creates a highly uncertain pro forma figure.

## Credit Agreement / Indenture

### Critical Red Flags
- **No maintenance covenants**: A fully covenant-lite structure with no springing maintenance provides no early warning mechanism for credit deterioration. The lender has no recourse until default on payment or other extreme events.
- **Unlimited or uncapped EBITDA addback baskets**: If the definition of Consolidated EBITDA includes uncapped addbacks for cost savings, synergies, or "run-rate" adjustments, the covenant metrics become meaningless. The company can always adjust EBITDA upward to avoid a covenant breach.
- **Weak anti-LME provisions**: If the credit agreement does not adequately protect against uptier transactions (Serta-style), dropdown financing (J.Crew-style), or non-pro-rata exchanges, the lender faces structural subordination risk without recourse.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Aggressive EBITDA definition**: Addbacks for projected cost savings (with or without caps), synergy addbacks for future acquisitions, unlimited management adjustment addbacks. Compare the EBITDA definition to market standard — if it is materially broader, the headline leverage ratio understates actual leverage.
- **Massive grower baskets**: Baskets for restricted payments, investments, or incremental debt that grow with EBITDA (or Total Assets) can become enormous as the company grows. A "greater of $50M and 100% of Consolidated EBITDA" basket at a company with $300M EBITDA is a $300M basket — enough for a meaningful dividend recap.
- **Weak collateral and guarantor structure**: Excluded subsidiaries that hold material assets or revenue, collateral limited to equity pledges (no asset liens), or foreign subsidiary exclusions when the business is primarily international.
- **Broad permitted investment and acquisition baskets**: Ability to make unlimited investments in unrestricted subsidiaries, acquire companies without leverage tests, or upstream cash to non-guarantor affiliates.

### Yellow Flags
- **Loose amendment and waiver thresholds**: Simple majority (50.1%) for material amendments rather than 66.7% or higher, allowing a small group of lenders to modify terms.
- **Short equity cure period**: Equity cure provisions that are generous in amount (unlimited cure capacity) and frequency (every quarter) reduce the protective value of maintenance covenants.
- **Portability provisions**: Terms that allow the credit facility to remain in place through a change of control without lender consent.

## Customer Data

### Critical Red Flags
- **High customer concentration**: Single customer >15% of revenue or top 5 customers >50% of revenue without long-term contracts. Loss of the top customer could trigger a covenant breach or liquidity crisis.
- **Customer in financial distress**: If a material customer is itself distressed (public filings show deteriorating financials, bankruptcy risk), the credit faces a double risk — revenue loss and potential bad debt expense.
- **Short-term or terminable-at-will contracts with concentrated customers**: The combination of high concentration and low contractual protection is the worst case.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Deteriorating customer retention**: Annual retention declining from 95% to 85% over 3 years indicates the product or service is losing value or competitors are gaining share.
- **Contract renewal cliff**: Multiple material contracts expiring in the same 12-month period creates concentrated renewal risk.
- **Revenue mix shifting to lower-quality customers**: New customer growth replacing lost revenue from higher-quality, longer-tenure customers.

## Legal Documents

### Critical Red Flags
- **Material litigation with quantifiable exposure**: Pending cases with potential exposure exceeding 0.5x EBITDA that are not adequately reserved. Includes mass tort, class action, and government enforcement cases.
- **Regulatory investigation**: Active investigation by DOJ, SEC, FTC, state AG, or industry-specific regulators. Investigations can result in fines, consent orders, business restrictions, and reputational damage.
- **Pending government action**: Government enforcement actions that could restrict the company's ability to operate (license revocation, product recalls, import/export restrictions).
- **IP challenges**: Patent infringement claims against the company's core products or services, especially if the patent holder has a track record of successful enforcement.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Change of control triggers in material contracts**: Customer or vendor contracts that terminate upon change of control, potentially disrupting revenue or supply chain post-transaction.
- **Undisclosed litigation**: Cases not included in the litigation summary that are discoverable through court records (PACER). Omission may be intentional.
- **Regulatory compliance gaps**: Missing or expired permits, outstanding compliance violations, or self-disclosed deficiencies.

## Environmental Documents

### Critical Red Flags
- **Recognized Environmental Conditions (RECs) without Phase II investigation**: RECs identified in a Phase I that have not been investigated with subsurface sampling leave the magnitude of contamination unknown.
- **Incomplete Phase I ESA**: Phase I that does not cover all material properties, uses outdated databases, or was performed by a non-qualified environmental professional.
- **Known contamination without remediation plan or cost estimate**: Identified contamination with no clear path to remediation and no cost estimate creates unbounded liability exposure.
- **Proximity to Superfund or state cleanup sites**: Potential for contribution claims under CERCLA, which imposes strict, joint and several liability regardless of fault.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Underground storage tanks (USTs)**: Current or historical USTs, especially for petroleum products. Even properly closed USTs can leave residual contamination.
- **Industrial use history**: Properties with historical use as dry cleaners, gas stations, manufacturing plants, or chemical storage facilities have elevated contamination risk.
- **PFAS exposure indicators**: Use of aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF), manufacturing or use of PFAS-containing products, discharge to water bodies subject to PFAS regulation.
- **Asbestos-containing materials**: Older buildings may contain asbestos in insulation, floor tiles, roofing. Abatement costs range from modest ($50K for limited areas) to substantial ($5M+ for comprehensive removal).

## Management and Governance

### Critical Red Flags
- **CFO departure (unplanned)**: The single most significant qualitative red flag. CFO departures frequently precede financial restatements, covenant violations, or material negative surprises. Investigate the stated reason, timing, and whether the departure was genuinely voluntary.
- **Multiple C-suite departures within 12 months**: Systemic management instability. May indicate internal conflict, culture problems, or undisclosed business challenges.
- **Litigation involving officers personally**: SEC enforcement actions, fraud allegations, or regulatory proceedings involving current officers suggest governance and integrity concerns.

### Significant Red Flags
- **Non-standard compensation structures**: Compensation that is disproportionate to company size, heavily weighted toward cash (no equity alignment), or tied to metrics that can be easily manipulated.
- **Controller/VP Finance turnover**: While less visible than C-suite departures, turnover at the controller level can indicate pressure to produce aggressive financial reports or disagreement with accounting practices.
- **Board composed entirely of sponsor appointees**: No independent directors means no independent oversight of management or sponsor behavior.
- **Excessive management company or advisory fees**: Fees paid to the sponsor that are disproportionate to services provided, especially if they escalate annually regardless of company performance.

### Yellow Flags
- **Key-person risk**: A single executive is essential to the business (customer relationships, technical expertise, regulatory relationships) with no identified successor.
- **Glassdoor ratings below 3.0**: Low employee satisfaction often correlates with operational challenges, turnover costs, and difficulty attracting talent.
- **Delayed SEC filings**: Filing extensions for 10-K or 10-Q, even within the permitted window, may signal accounting difficulties or internal control issues.
