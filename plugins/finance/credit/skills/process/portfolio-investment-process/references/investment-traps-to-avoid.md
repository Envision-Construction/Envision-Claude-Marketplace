## Investment Traps to Avoid

### 1. The Yield Trap

**Definition**: Buying a bond because of its high yield, without ensuring the credit quality justifies it.

**Example**:
```
Bond A: High-Yield credit, 8% yield, overleveraged, deteriorating margins
Bond B: Solid-grade credit, 4.5% yield, stable business, improving trends

Yield-chasing investor buys Bond A for the extra 350 bp.
Six months later, margins contract further. Bond spreads from 300 to 500 bp.
Bond A drops 8% in price. Total return: -8% + 4% coupon = -4%.
Bond B tightens to 250 bp. Bond B appreciates 2%. Total return: 2% + 4.5% = 6.5%.
```

**How to avoid:**
- Don't start with yield; start with credit quality
- Ask: "If the yield is 8%, why? What credit risk am I taking?"
- Compare realized returns (price change + coupon) across multiple scenarios
- Prefer boring, high-quality bonds over exciting, high-yield bonds unless risk/reward is truly favorable

### 2. The Complacency Trap

**Definition**: Assuming low yields or high prices mean low risk. "If bond is trading at 95, the market says it's safe."

**Reality**: Prices can move for reasons unrelated to credit—flow, technicals, Fed policy.

**Example:**
```
Bond trading at 99.5, yield only 3.5%, spreads 150 bp (very tight)
Investor assumes the market has it right. Buys bond "to hold to maturity."
Two quarters later, company misses earnings. Spreads spike to 300 bp.
Bond drops 5 points (price declines to 94.5). Though still "safe," it's down 5%.
```

**How to avoid:**
- Always do your own credit analysis; don't rely on price to tell you if something is safe
- Just because spreads are tight doesn't mean they can't widen
- Monitor credit fundamentals independently; don't assume the market has discovered everything
- Be especially skeptical of "consensus" views; if everyone agrees something is low-risk, they're probably all positioned the same way

### 3. The Model Extrapolation Trap

**Definition**: Projecting past financial trends into the future without stress scenarios.

**Example:**
```
A company has grown EBITDA 8% annually for the last 4 years.
Analyst projects EBITDA growing 8% for the next 3 years.
Leverage is forecast to decline from 4.0x to 3.0x on this growth.

But the company operates in a cyclical industry and is near peak margins.
A recession hits; EBITDA declines 15% instead of growing 8%.
Leverage spikes to 6.5x, not declining to 3.0x.
```

**How to avoid:**
- Build multiple scenarios: base, upside, downside, severe stress
- Test sensitivity: "If revenue declines X%, does the credit still work?"
- Challenge assumptions: "Why should this growth rate persist? What could disrupt it?"
- Be especially skeptical of margin expansion assumptions (usually mean revert)
- Look for mean reversion: High margins often compress; negative FCF sometimes reverses

### 4. The Hard Asset Bias Trap

**Definition**: Overweighting tangible assets (real estate, equipment) and underweighting cash generation.

**In reality**: A business's value comes from cash flow, not the assets it owns.

**Example:**
```
Company A: Cable operator with "hidden value" in owned fiber routes (hard assets)
Company B: Software company with high margins, recurring revenue, minimal assets

Hard-asset bias investors prefer Company A because of the tangible assets.
Cable industry disruption hits; margins compress; refinancing becomes difficult.
Company B's recurring revenue is stable; it refinances easily.
Despite having no real assets, Company B is the safer credit.
```

**How to avoid:**
- Prioritize cash flow generation over asset ownership
- Ask: "What's the cash generation ability in a stressed scenario?"
- Realize that assets can become stranded (utility stranded nuclear plant, pipeline not needed)
- Focus on **earning power**, not balance sheet asset values

### 5. Over-Reliance on Quantitative Screens

**Definition**: Using relative value "sorts" (leverage ratios, yield spreads) and ignoring structural/event risks.

**Example:**
```
Relative value screen ranks bonds by lowest STW / (Debt/EBITDA) ratio.
Screen picks Company A because it has the tightest "spread per turn" of leverage.

But the screen misses:
- Major customer concentration (80% revenue from one customer)
- Upcoming maturity wall (50% of debt due in next 24 months)
- Deteriorating operating trends (margins down each quarter)
- Activist shareholder (pushing for LBO at lower valuation)

These "soft" factors matter more than the mechanical spread ratio.
```

**How to avoid:**
- Use screens to identify opportunities, not to make final decisions
- Always dig deeper: Read the prospectus, listen to earnings calls, talk to management
- Look for red flags in structure (maturity walls, customer concentration, leverage step-ups)
- Consider event risk that quant screens miss
- Combine quantitative ranking with qualitative judgment

### 6. The Lemming Effect

**Definition**: When everyone has the same investment thesis, someone isn't thinking independently.

**Example:**
```
Consensus in 2006: "Housing will recover; mortgage bonds are safe."
Consensus in 2019: "Rates are going lower forever; duration is safe."
Consensus in 2021: "Tech IPOs are all winners; growth will continue."

Crowds often form around the most comfortable thesis, not the most accurate one.
```

**How to avoid:**
- Be suspicious of unanimous views; ask "What is everyone missing?"
- Seek contrarian data: Institutional positioning, short interest, insider selling
- Build conviction from independent analysis, not from consensus
- Consider: "If I'm the only one seeing this credit risk, am I crazy or are others overlooking it?"
- Be especially cautious in crowded trades (all investors have same position)
