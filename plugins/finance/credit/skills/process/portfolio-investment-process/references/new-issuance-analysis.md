## Part 3: New Issuance Analysis

### Information Asymmetry: New Issues as a Learning Tool

New issues (bonds, loans, or equity) provide more detailed information than ongoing coverage because:

1. **Roadshow materials**: Management presents comprehensive slides on business model, markets, financials
2. **Bank book** (for loans/private deals): Can include forward-looking projections and aggressive EBITDA adjustments
3. **Prospectus** (for public bonds): SEC-required document with risk factors, historical financials, structure

**Key difference**: Bank books can include "management case" projections; prospectuses are official documents and typically include only historical data and high-level guidance.

### Key Sections to Read in a New Issue Prospectus

1. **Use of Proceeds**
   - Where is the money going? Acquisition, refinancing, working capital?
   - If it's an acquisition, are you getting a copy of the acquisition agreement? Can you see the target's financials?

2. **Risk Factors**
   - What does management think could go wrong?
   - Ranked by management's view of severity
   - Often reveals structural vulnerabilities or market headwinds

3. **Business Description**
   - How does the company make money?
   - Who are the customers? How concentrated is revenue?
   - What's the competitive position?

4. **Management's Discussion & Analysis (MD&A)**
   - Why did financial results change period-to-period?
   - Management's view of trends and headwinds

5. **Financial Statements**
   - At least 2-3 years of audited or pro forma financials
   - Footnotes often contain important structural details

6. **Structure & Terms**
   - Debt capitalization table: what debt is senior, what is subordinated?
   - Covenant language: leverage tests, interest coverage, asset sale sweeps
   - Call features: when can the debt be refinanced?
   - Baskets and carve-outs: exceptions to covenants (e.g., can add debt if leverage < 3.0x)

7. **Related Party / Affiliate Transactions**
   - Are the sponsors taking fees? What's the management fee burden?
   - Are there related-party supply contracts at off-market prices?

### Pro Forma Adjustments: Separating Fact from Fiction

New issue documents include "Adjusted EBITDA" or "Pro Forma EBITDA" that may differ significantly from reported EBITDA.

**Common add-backs to be skeptical of:**

1. **Stock-based compensation**: Legitimate add-back (non-cash), but note it still represents dilution
2. **Restructuring charges**: One-time or recurring? If recurring, maybe it's part of normal business
3. **Management fees**: Might recur; unclear if truly non-recurring
4. **Deferred revenue adjustments**: Complex accounting; understand what's being adjusted
5. **Cost synergies** (in acquisitions): "We will save $20M in year 2"—will you really?
6. **Estimated costs** (for new operations): Unproven; could overrun

**Red flag**: When adjustments are larger than the reported number.

**Example:**
- Reported EBITDA: ($90.6M) — negative!
- Add-backs: $225.2M for various items
- Adjusted EBITDA: $134.6M

This company is burning cash operationally but using accounting adjustments to appear profitable. Be very cautious about investing in negative reported cash flow.

### New Issue Pricing Analysis

**Strong market conditions**: New issuers price bonds at tight spreads (low yield), often near or inside the spreads of existing bonds from comparable companies.

**Soft market conditions**: New issuers must offer a "new issue discount"—spreads several tens of basis points wider than existing comparable bonds—to attract investors.

**How to use this:**
- If new issue prices inside existing bonds, the market is saying the new issue is better quality (or the old bond is stale/illiquid)
- If new issue prices outside by 50+ bp, either the new issue is weaker, or the new issue offers a bargain

### Acquisition Price Analysis

For LBO or acquisition financings, compare:

1. **EBITDA multiple paid** (acquisition price ÷ EBITDA) vs.
2. **Trading multiples** of public comparables
3. **Prior transaction multiples** for similar deals

**Example:**
- New issue: acquisition of MediCo at $500M on $50M EBITDA = 10.0x multiple
- Public comparables: trading at 8.5x-9.0x
- Prior acquisitions: 8.5x-9.5x
- Conclusion: **Buyer is paying a premium** (10.0x vs 8.5-9.5x range)

This might indicate:
- Strong synergies the buyer can achieve
- Seller had multiple bidders (competitive process)
- Buyer overpaid (risky for debt investors—more leverage to justify the price)

### Proxy Statement Insight

If the acquisition target is a public company, review the proxy statement (Def 14A) filed with the SEC. It often includes:

- **Other bids received** in the auction process—shows the valuation range
- **Fairness opinions** from investment banks (note: banks are paid by the acquiring company, so fairness opinions are somewhat self-serving)
- **Change of control fees** and severance—these are costs of the deal

**Example:**
- Winner bid: $500M (10.0x)
- Second bid: $475M (9.5x)
- Third bid: $450M (9.0x)

The winner paid 5.3% more than the second-place bidder. Was it worth the leverage risk? Depends on synergies.
