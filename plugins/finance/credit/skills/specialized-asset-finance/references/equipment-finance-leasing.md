## Equipment Finance and Leasing

Equipment finance sits between pure cash-flow lending and pure collateral lending. The lender is underwriting both the obligor's willingness and ability to keep paying and the asset's ability to preserve value if the obligor fails.

## Lease Form Matters

### Finance Lease

In a finance lease, the lessee bears most of the economics of ownership. Credit analysis should focus on:

- the lessee's fixed payment burden
- whether the lease term effectively consumes the asset's useful life
- whether the end-of-term buyout or residual assumption is realistic

### Operating Lease

In an operating lease, the lessor retains more residual exposure. Credit analysis should focus on:

- how essential the asset is to the lessee
- whether the lease can be rejected or renegotiated in distress
- whether the lessor can realistically re-lease or remarket the asset

Regardless of accounting treatment, lease liabilities should be treated as real fixed obligations in leverage and fixed-charge analysis.

## Sale-Leasebacks

A sale-leaseback monetizes an owned asset while leaving the operator in possession. Treat it as both a financing event and a transfer of asset economics.

Key underwriting questions:

1. Is the rent supportable relative to the asset and the operator's cash flow?
2. Is the asset mission-critical, or can the lessee walk away in stress?
3. Who bears maintenance, insurance, tax, and return-condition obligations?
4. Does the transaction improve liquidity at the cost of weaker long-term flexibility?

If a sale-leaseback solves a near-term liquidity problem but leaves the operator with a heavier fixed-charge burden, it can be credit-negative despite appearing to delever the balance sheet.

## Structuring Principles

### Match Amortization to Asset Decay

Debt should amortize in a way that keeps collateral coverage intact as the asset ages. The faster the asset can lose value, the less comfort the lender should take from a distant balloon.

### Treat Balloon Risk as Underwriting Risk

Balloon or residual structures are viable only when:

- the asset has a credible end-of-term value
- a refinance market should still exist at maturity
- the lender is not relying on optimistic maintenance or demand assumptions

### Use Flexibility Carefully

Master leases, seasonal payment structures, and schedule-based additions can be useful, but they increase the need for:

- clear asset tracking
- consistent perfection and title control
- disciplined reporting on utilization, maintenance, and remarketing exposure

## Residual Value Risk

Residual value is the lender's or lessor's exposure to what the asset will actually realize at the end of the structure. It depends less on accounting labels than on real-world asset behavior.

### Main Drivers

- useful life remaining at lease or loan maturity
- maintenance condition and service history
- technological obsolescence
- regulatory compliance and future-use restrictions
- depth of the secondary market
- correlation between asset value and the obligor's operating cycle

### Risk Mitigants

- conservative residual assumptions
- shorter lease terms for fast-changing assets
- maintenance covenants and reserve structures
- remarketing rights and repossession readiness
- portfolio diversification across assets, vintages, and end markets
