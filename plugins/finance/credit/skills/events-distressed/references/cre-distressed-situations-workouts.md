## CRE Distressed Situations & Workouts

### CRE Distress Triggers

Commercial real estate loans default for different reasons than corporate debt. Key triggers:

**Maturity Default (Refinancing Risk)**
- Most common CRE default trigger in a higher-rate or lower-liquidity environment
- Borrower cannot refinance a maturing loan on terms the property can support
- Example: a loan originated at a low coupon matures into a materially higher-rate market
  - Old debt service may have been supportable under prior rates
  - New debt service may double or otherwise rise beyond sustainable NOI support
  - If in-place NOI cannot support the reset debt service, refinancing becomes uneconomic or unavailable
- Alternative: property value declining due to higher cap rates, so max loan proceeds = $8M instead of $10M
  - Shortfall: $2M cash required to refinance
- Rising rate environment → extended maturities → forced sales or refinancing shortfalls

**Cash Flow Default (NOI Decline)**
- Property underperforms: occupancy drops, rents decline, expenses rise
- DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio) = NOI / annual debt service
  - Loan requires minimum DSCR 1.25x
  - If NOI declines 30%, DSCR may drop to 1.0x or below
  - Triggers covenant breach → default

**Tenant Bankruptcy or Departure (Anchor Tenant Loss)**
- Single-tenant property (corporate user building, triple-net lease)
  - Anchor tenant bankruptcy removes 100% of rent
  - Immediate NOI = 0 (or low subleasing rents)
  - Example: Medical office building 100% leased to practice that files Chapter 11 → rent stops
- Anchor tenant in shopping center (Target, Sears anchor)
  - Large vacancy removes 30-40% of center rent
  - Shadow losses: other tenants may terminate if anchor closes (co-tenancy clauses)
  - Rapid NOI collapse

**Market Cycle Downturn**
- Cap rates expand (property values collapse)
- Example: Office cap rates expand from 5% to 8% due to rising rates and oversupply
  - Property worth $1,000,000 at 5% cap rate ($50K NOI) → $625,000 at 8% cap rate
  - LTV (Loan-to-Value) = Loan balance / Property value
  - If loan = $700K, LTV was 70% → now 112% (negative equity)
  - Refinancing impossible (underwater)
  - Borrower walks away (non-recourse loan)

**Construction Delay or Cost Overrun**
- Development loan: builder runs out of money before completion
- Lender must fund additional draws to finish project (or foreclose on incomplete property worth nothing)
- Pressure to modify terms, extend funding, or restructure deal

### CRE Workout Options

**Loan Modification (Out-of-Court)**
- Lender and borrower negotiate new terms:
  - Interest rate reduction: Lower rate to improve DSCR
  - Term extension: Extend maturity 2-3 years for refinancing window
  - Principal forbearance: Waive some interest/principal for period
  - Amortization waiver: Defer principal payments until property stabilizes
  - Covenant relief: Relax DSCR, LTV, or other financial covenants
- Pros: Preserves borrower/lender relationship, avoids foreclosure costs, faster resolution
- Cons: Lender takes haircut; borrower still on hook for full debt eventually
- Timeline: weeks to months (faster than foreclosure or bankruptcy)

**Deed-in-Lieu of Foreclosure (DL)**
- Borrower voluntarily transfers property title to lender
- Lender takes possession without foreclosure process
- Borrower: avoids foreclosure costs and timeline, walks away clean
- Lender: acquires REO quickly, avoids 6-18 month foreclosure timeline
- Requirements:
  - Title must be clear (no junior liens or other impediments)
  - Lender must agree (willing to take property in current condition)
  - Property must have sufficient value (lender won't accept property with environmental liability)
- Example: $8M loan, property worth $7M, borrower offers deed-in-lieu → lender takes property, absorbs $1M loss immediately

**Discounted Payoff (DPO)**
- Lender accepts less than full loan balance as final payment
- Borrower finds cash or alternate lender to pay the discount
- Example: $10M loan, lender agrees to accept $7M cash payoff
- Used when: borrower wants quick exit, third-party investor buys note at discount
- Typical in non-recourse loans where lender cannot chase borrower (can only take property)
- Recovery: 70% (vs. potential 40-50% in foreclosure/bankruptcy)

**Note Sale**
- Lender sells loan to third party (distressed debt investor)
- Buyer purchases at discount: buys $10M loan for $6M
- Buyer gains rights to collect from borrower and foreclose on property
- Lender accepts loss immediately (vs. carrying loan through default)
- Buyer now owns distressed position; can modify, foreclose, or work with borrower

**Partial Release / Collateral Sale**
- Borrower sells portion of collateral property
- Proceeds applied to partial loan paydown
- Remaining loan reduced and modified on smaller collateral base
- Example: 50-acre shopping center; sell 10 acres for $2M → reduce loan from $12M to $10M
- Allows borrower to survive by reducing debt burden

**A/B Note Split (Restructuring Strategy)**
- Senior A-Note: Reduces loan to sustainable level (backed by senior lien on property)
  - Example: A-Note = $6M at 4%, amortized over 15 years (DSCR supportable)
  - Priority: paid first from property cash flow
- Junior B-Note: Deferred principal balance
  - Example: B-Note = $4M (original $10M loan minus $6M A-Note)
  - Subordinated: paid only after A-Note fully paid
  - Terms: defer interest/principal or PIK (accrete at 5% annually)
  - Structure: potential equity conversion if property appreciates significantly
- Effect: Preserves lender's senior position while deferring junior payment (borrower breathing room)

### Foreclosure Process

**Judicial Foreclosure** (court-supervised process)
- Used in states requiring court approval (most states)
- Timeline: 6-18 months depending on jurisdiction and court backlog
- Process:
  1. Notice of default filed
  2. Redemption period (borrower can cure default) 3-6 months
  3. Foreclosure lawsuit filed by lender
  4. Judgment of foreclosure obtained from court
  5. Property sold at public auction (trustee sale)
- Borrower rights:
  - Right to cure default during redemption period
  - Right of redemption (post-sale): some states allow borrower to repurchase property within 6-12 months post-auction (at full sale price)
- Deficiency judgment: If property sells for less than loan balance, borrower liable for deficiency (in recourse states)
  - Example: $10M loan, property sells for $7M → $3M deficiency
  - Borrower personally liable for $3M (if recourse loan)
  - Lender can garnish wages, levy bank accounts, pursue other remedies

**Non-Judicial Foreclosure (Power of Sale)**
- Used in states allowing non-judicial sales (trust deed states: CA, TX, NV, WA, etc.)
- Timeline: 2-6 months (faster than judicial)
- Process:
  1. Notice of default + notice of sale published
  2. 30-45 day cure period (shorter than judicial)
  3. Trustee (neutral third party) auctions property at sale location
  4. Highest bidder takes title (typically lender credit bids)
- No court involvement, faster timeline
- Borrower limited deficiency rights (non-recourse effectively for most states)
- Example: HELOC foreclosures often non-judicial (fast and efficient)

**Lender Credit Bidding**
- In foreclosure auction, lender bids using loan balance (not cash)
- Example: $10M loan, property at auction
  - Lender "credit bids" $10M (using loan as currency)
  - No outside bid higher → lender takes property at $10M credit bid price
  - Lender takes title; absorbs the gap between $10M bid and $8M property value ($2M loss)
- Critical strategy: If property has value above loan, lender bids up to preserve value (vs. accepting sale to third party)
- If property worth less than loan: lender bids loan amount (accepts REO ownership and loss)

**Receivership (Court-Appointed Management)**
- Court appoints neutral receiver to manage property during workout
- Used when: parties in dispute, property deteriorating, borrower cannot/will not maintain
- Receiver duties:
  - Collect rents from tenants
  - Pay operating expenses, taxes, insurance
  - Maintain property
  - Distribute cash from operations per court order
- Cash waterfall: typically operating expenses → property taxes/insurance → senior lender → junior creditors
- Timeline: temporary measure (usually 6-24 months) until foreclosure or resolution
- Cost: receiver fees (1-2% of gross rents) carved from cash flow

### CMBS Special Servicing

Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities (CMBS) loans have special servicers (not standard loan servicers).

**Transfer to Special Servicing Triggers**
Loan transferred from master servicer to special servicer upon:
- 60+ day monetary default (missed payment)
- Imminent default (projected within 120 days)
- Covenant default (non-monetary, e.g., LTV breach, DSCR failure)
- Bankruptcy filing
- Maturity default (cannot refinance, must foreclose or modify)

**Special Servicer Authority**
Per Pooling & Servicing Agreement (PSA), special servicer can:
- Modify loans (rate, term, amortization)
- Extend maturity
- Foreclose and liquidate
- Sell the loan (to third party)
- Approve discounted payoffs (DPO)
- Subject to: Controlling class approval and PSA restrictions

**Special Servicer Fees**
- Annual servicing fee: 0.25% of loan balance (routine administration)
- Workout fee: 1% of loan balance (for modifications, extensions, restructuring)
- Liquidation fee: 1% of net proceeds recovered (for foreclosure, sale)
- Incentive: servicer earnings increase with successful workout (vs. liquidation loss)

**Controlling Class Concept**
- Most junior bond class still receiving principal payments controls special servicer decisions
- Example: AAA, AA, A, BBB classes all receiving distributions; BB class taking loss
  - BB class is controlling class (junior-most with principal)
  - BB class votes on special servicer approval of modifications
  - AA class cannot override BB (even though subordinated to BB in waterfall)
- Creates tension: junior class wants aggressive modifications (preserve equity), senior wants quick liquidation (minimize loss)

**Appraisal Reduction Events**
- If property appraised value falls below certain threshold vs. outstanding debt:
  - Controlling class may shift to more senior class
  - Appraisal reduction → principal reduction for controlling class → control shifts down
  - Example: Originally outstanding debt = $200M, AAA-M = $80M (controlling)
    - If appraisal decline → controlling class shifts to AA (new most junior with principal)
    - AA now controls servicer decisions (may be more conservative than AAA-M)

**REMIC Tax Rule Modifications**
- Real Estate Mortgage Investment Conduit (REMIC) tax treatment requires compliance with modification rules
- 2-year safe harbor: certain modifications allowed without losing REMIC status
  - Rate/term changes, maturity extensions, amortization schedule changes
  - Servicer can act without REMIC concern if within safe harbor
- After 2 years: modifications may trigger REMIC disqualification (tax liability for investors)
- PSA language typically includes: "Servicer not liable for REMIC consequences of modifications per agreement"

### REO Management

When lender takes title post-foreclosure, lender becomes property owner (Real Estate Owned).

**Lender Responsibilities Post-Foreclosure**
- Property tax liability: lender now liable (tax bills come to lender)
- Insurance: lender must maintain comprehensive coverage
- Operating costs: property taxes, insurance, utilities (if occupied)
- Environmental liability: owner (now lender) liable for CERCLA violations
  - Phase I Environmental Site Assessment recommended upon acquisition
  - If contamination found: lender may face multi-million dollar remediation costs
  - May need environmental insurance or third-party indemnification

**Stabilize and Disposition Strategy**
- Stabilize occupancy: re-lease vacant units/spaces
- Stabilize operations: address deferred maintenance, bring current on taxes/insurance
- Reduce carrying costs: sell quickly (every month costs money)
- Timeline: 6-24 months to stabilize and sell (varies by market, property type)

**REO Holding Costs**
- Property taxes: full annual liability
- Insurance: increased rates (REO status, potentially vacant)
- Utilities: property must be maintained (climate controlled to prevent damage)
- Maintenance/R&M: prevent further deterioration
- Management: coordinate leasing, tenant relations, repairs
- Total: 2-5% of property value annually (can be substantial drag on recovery)
- Example: $10M property in foreclosure → $200K-500K annual holding costs

**Sale Execution**
- Broker selection: local market expertise (for local properties)
- Marketing period: 3-6 months typical
- Timeline: list, market, negotiate, underwriting, closing (total 6-12 months)
- Recovery rate: usually 5-15% below fair market value (fire-sale discount)
  - Non-recourse loans: lender absorbs gap (cannot pursue borrower)
  - Recourse loans: deficiency judgment against borrower (if proceeds < balance)

### CRE Recovery Analysis

**Recovery from Collateral Value**
Unlike corporate bankruptcy (where recovery depends on enterprise value and going-concern value), CRE recovery is heavily collateral-dependent:
- Property value (via comparable sales, appraisals, cap rates)
- Forced liquidation timing (faster = lower price)
- Market conditions (distressed sales in weak markets fetch lower prices)
- Environmental or structural issues (reduce value further)

**Valuation in Distress**
- Underperforming property (high vacancy, below-market rents)
  - Value = stabilized NOI / market cap rate
  - Example: current NOI = $300K but stabilized would be $500K (after re-leasing)
  - Stabilized value at 6% cap rate = $500K / 0.06 = $8.3M
  - But if foreclosing during distress, buyer may apply higher cap rate (distressed seller premium)
  - Distressed cap rate: 7-8% (vs. market 6%) → value = $6.25M-6.67M
- Comparable sales approach: similar properties sold recently (adjusted for condition)
- Cost approach: land value + replacement cost (rarely used for distressed valuations)

**Timeline Impact**
- Rushed foreclosure (quick sale): property may fetch 10-20% below fair market
- 12-month stabilization period before sale: recover 5-10% premium vs. immediate sale
- Extended holding (24+ months): carrying costs offset value improvement

**CRE vs. Corporate Recovery: Key Difference**
- Corporate bankruptcy: enterprise value can grow through restructuring (business improvements, cost cuts)
- CRE distress: recovery = collateral value only (property doesn't improve without capital investment)
  - REO owner (lender) must invest in TI, leasing, marketing to improve property
  - If lender invests capital: property value grows, recovery improves
  - If lender liquidates quickly: recovery = current market value (discounted)
- Example: $10M loan on property worth $7M in distressed condition
  - Quick REO sale: recover $7M (30% loss)
  - 18-month stabilization (invest $1M in TI, leasing, marketing) then sell for $8.5M: recover $8.5M (15% loss)
  - Servicer decision: hold and invest vs. quick liquidation → depends on property, market, interest rates

**Historical CRE Recovery Rates**
See `references/default-recovery-rates.md` for historical CRE loss severity by loan type:
- Senior mortgages (first lien): 60-80% recovery typical
- Mezzanine loans: 20-40% recovery typical
- Construction loans (incomplete): 10-30% recovery typical
- Market-dependent: strong markets → higher recovery, weak markets (2008-2009) → lower recovery
