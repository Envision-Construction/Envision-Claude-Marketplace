## DIP Financing Framework

Debtor-in-possession financing is not just a liquidity bridge. It is a control instrument that can determine case direction, creditor leverage, and how much distributable value remains for pre-petition claims.

### 1. Start with the Purpose

Ask why the DIP exists:

- **New money DIP**: Funds ongoing operations, working capital, and professional fees
- **Roll-up DIP**: Elevates some or all pre-petition claims into post-petition priority
- **Hybrid DIP**: Provides new liquidity while also improving pre-petition lender position

The analytical question is not simply "is there a DIP?" It is "who gains priority, who gains control, and what value is moved ahead of the existing stack?"

### 2. Core Legal Mechanics

- DIP financing is approved under Section 364 of the Bankruptcy Code
- Superpriority status places the DIP ahead of ordinary administrative claims
- Priming liens allow the DIP to rank ahead of existing liens if the court finds adequate protection
- Adequate protection can include replacement liens, current-pay interest, equity cushion, or a fallback superpriority claim

### 3. Why DIP Structure Matters for Recovery

### New money DIP

- Usually preserves the pre-petition stack more cleanly than a roll-up
- Dilutes junior recoveries by adding a new senior claim, but often supports going-concern value
- Can still improve overall creditor outcomes if it buys time for an orderly resolution

### Roll-up DIP

- Pulls pre-petition claims into the most senior part of the waterfall
- Compresses value available to non-participating creditors and junior classes
- Often signals defensive positioning by existing lenders and a more contested process

### Hybrid DIP

- Requires separate analysis of the genuine liquidity benefit and the priority transfer
- Often looks balanced on paper while still meaningfully shifting leverage toward the DIP group

### 4. Lender Protections to Review

- **Budget covenant**: The DIP budget often governs weekly case flexibility more than headline pricing
- **Milestones**: Plan filing, sale, confirmation, or emergence deadlines can force a specific path
- **Termination events**: Budget misses, milestone breaches, or governance changes can hand control to the DIP lenders
- **Challenge period**: Determines how long committees can contest pre-petition liens or claims
- **Carve-outs**: Protect professional fees and other estate functions so the case can still run after a default
- **Exit conversion rights**: Give DIP lenders a path into the post-emergence capital structure

### 5. Questions for Underwriting

1. How much of the DIP is true new money versus rolled-up pre-petition debt?
2. What collateral is primed, and who loses value as a result?
3. Is the DIP enabling a credible plan, or simply locking in lender advantage before a fight?
4. What milestones or budget controls effectively determine the outcome?
5. After DIP claims, fees, and carve-outs, what value is left for the rest of the stack?

### 6. Common Mistakes

- Treating DIP pricing as the main issue instead of priority transfer and control
- Assuming all DIPs are equally creditor-friendly because they support liquidity
- Ignoring the difference between new money and roll-up economics
- Modeling recoveries without adjusting the waterfall for DIP superpriority and carve-outs
- Missing how a milestone package can force a sale or plan that benefits one creditor group over another

### Claims Arising from DIP Financing

#### Bankruptcy Expenses: Super-Priority Claims

**Cost of Bankruptcy**:
- Lawyers: $3-15M+ depending on complexity
- Accountants: $1-5M
- Financial advisors: $1-10M (investment bankers advising on asset sales, valuations, restructuring alternatives)
- Administrative costs: court fees, printing, noticing
- Total: often $10-50M+ for large Chapter 11

**Super-Priority Status**:
- These expenses are paid FIRST, before all other creditors
- Come from estate funds immediately
- "Super" = senior to even secured debt (with limited exceptions)
- Must be approved by bankruptcy judge (monitors for excessive spending)

**Cost Control**:
- Judge can require cost budgets
- Committees of creditors (official creditors committee) monitor expenses
- Fees capped or approved in advance

#### DIP Loan Sizing and Providers

**Sizing DIP Loan**:
- Estimate from operational cash burn (monthly operating expenses)
- Working capital needs (inventory, receivables)
- Professional fees and other bankruptcy costs
- Expected bankruptcy length (usually 12-24 months)
- Example: $1M monthly burn x 18 months + $15M professional fees + $5M working capital = $38M DIP

**Who Provides DIP?**
- Existing bank lenders (bank revolvers often convert to DIP)
- New lenders (distressed lenders, CLOs)
- Sponsor equity (if sponsor still invested)
- DIP market: specialized lenders who understand bankruptcy workings

**Lender Position**:
- First-out priority (gets paid before restructuring)
- Secured by all unencumbered assets (and can take priming lien on encumbered assets in some cases)
- Attractive risk/reward: super-priority + short duration
- Rates: typically 6-12% depending on risk
- Fees: 2-5% upfront, 1-2% success fees

#### Cash Buildup During Bankruptcy

**Value Creation**:
- If company is generating positive cash flow, cash builds up during bankruptcy
- This cash is available for distribution to creditors (increases the pie)
- Example: $100M DIP loan received at filing, company generates $15M cumulative positive EBITDA during 18-month bankruptcy = $115M available after DIP repayment
