## Ratings Agencies & Credit Assessment

### Why Ratings Matter

Ratings are a third-party shorthand for relative credit risk. They help frame:

- the likely investor base for a financing;
- which mandates can own the security;
- how much spread compensation the market may demand; and
- how a credit may behave if it migrates across rating categories.

They are a starting point, not a substitute for independent underwriting.

### Major Agency Scales

The three most commonly referenced global agencies are:

- **S&P**
- **Moody's**
- **Fitch**

Their scales differ in notation but map to the same broad idea:

| Broad Category | S&P / Fitch | Moody's |
|---|---|---|
| Highest quality | AAA | Aaa |
| High quality | AA | Aa |
| Upper medium grade | A | A |
| Lowest investment grade | BBB | Baa |
| Speculative / high yield | BB, B, CCC and below | Ba, B, Caa and below |
| Default | D | C / defaulted obligation |

Use `references/rating-agency-thresholds.md` for current leverage, coverage, and sector-specific benchmark ranges. This local reference is intentionally conceptual rather than threshold-driven.

### Issuer vs. Issue Ratings

- **Issuer rating**: View of the borrower's general capacity to meet obligations.
- **Issue rating**: View of a specific instrument after considering seniority, collateral, guarantees, and structural subordination.

Issue ratings can differ from issuer ratings because recovery prospects differ across the capital structure.

### Outlooks, Watches, and Migration

- **Outlook**: Directional view over a medium-term horizon such as positive, stable, or negative.
- **Watch / review**: Higher-immediacy signal that a rating action may be coming after an identified event or review process.
- **Migration**: Movement between rating categories over time.
- **Fallen angel**: Issuer or bond downgraded from investment grade into high yield.
- **Rising star**: Issuer or bond upgraded from high yield into investment grade.

Migration matters because it can change the natural buyer base and trigger forced buying or selling by mandate-constrained investors.

### Split Ratings

When agencies disagree, the credit has a **split rating**. That is common and does not automatically signal distress. Differences usually reflect:

- different views on business risk, leverage tolerance, or event risk;
- different treatment of recovery and structural subordination; or
- different timing around upgrades and downgrades.

For covenant, investment-policy, or index purposes, the relevant governing document determines whether the highest, middle, or lowest rating controls.

### How Ratings Fit Into Underwriting

Ratings can be useful for:

- calibrating where a credit sits on the risk spectrum;
- comparing issuer ratings and issue ratings across a capital structure;
- anticipating market-access constraints; and
- framing downgrade or refinancing risk.

Ratings are weaker when:

- the issuer has unusual accounting, sponsor behavior, or event risk;
- documentation allows structural leakage not captured in headline metrics; or
- a sector or asset class has specialized drivers better handled by a dedicated skill.

### Practical Underwriting Principle

Treat ratings as an external check on your work:

1. understand the business and capital structure independently;
2. compare your conclusions to agency framing; and
3. investigate the gap if your view differs materially.
