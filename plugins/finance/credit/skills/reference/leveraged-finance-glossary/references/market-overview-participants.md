## Market Overview & Participants

### What Leveraged Finance Covers

Leveraged finance is the market for debt raised by issuers whose credit profile, leverage, business risk, or sponsorship typically places them outside plain-vanilla investment-grade financing. In practice it usually includes:

- **Leveraged loans**: Bank or institutional floating-rate loans, often senior secured.
- **High-yield bonds**: Public or private fixed-rate debt issued below investment grade.
- **Bridge and acquisition financing**: Interim debt used to fund transactions before longer-term capital is placed.
- **Fallen angels and crossover credits**: Issuers that move between investment-grade and non-investment-grade markets.

Use `references/rating-agency-thresholds.md` for current rating calibration and `references/market-benchmarks.md` for current market conditions.

### Core Market Functions

The market does four recurring jobs:

1. **Provide acquisition and refinancing capital** for leveraged issuers.
2. **Allocate risk across the capital structure** from secured loans through unsecured or subordinated debt.
3. **Transfer risk from originators to investors** through syndication, distribution, and trading.
4. **Reprice credit risk over time** through primary issuance and secondary-market marks.

### Participant Roles

#### Borrowers and Sponsors
- **Issuer / Borrower**: The operating company or financing vehicle raising debt.
- **Sponsor**: The private equity owner or strategic backer shaping leverage, structure, and financing strategy.

#### Arrangers and Intermediaries
- **Lead arranger / bookrunner**: Structures, markets, and allocates a syndicated loan or financing package.
- **Commercial bank**: Provides revolvers, relationship lending, and sometimes bridge commitments.
- **Dealer / sales desk**: Distributes securities and supports secondary trading.

#### Investors
- **CLOs and loan funds**: Core buyers of broadly syndicated institutional loans.
- **High-yield funds and separate accounts**: Core buyers of HY bonds and crossover risk.
- **Insurance companies, pensions, and long-only institutions**: Often favor income, seniority, and portfolio fit.
- **Hedge funds and distressed investors**: Trade event risk, restructurings, documentation dislocations, and stressed paper.
- **Private credit lenders**: Originate directly or participate in less broadly distributed transactions.

#### Control and Oversight Parties
- **Administrative agent**: Handles notices, calculations, payment flows, and lender records for loans.
- **Collateral agent / security trustee**: Holds liens or security interests for the lender group.
- **Indenture trustee**: Represents bondholders under the bond indenture.
- **Ratings agencies**: Publish issuer and issue ratings used by many investors as a reference point, not a substitute for analysis.

### Primary vs. Secondary Market

- **Primary market**: New financing is originated, underwritten, priced, and allocated.
- **Secondary market**: Existing loans and bonds trade between investors after issuance.

The primary market determines structure and economics at launch. The secondary market tests whether the original structure still clears based on evolving fundamentals, technicals, and liquidity.

### Enduring Distinctions That Matter

| Distinction | What It Usually Implies |
|---|---|
| **Loan vs. bond** | Floating-rate and lender-style documentation vs. fixed-rate and capital-markets documentation |
| **Secured vs. unsecured** | Different collateral access, recovery expectations, and covenant emphasis |
| **Public vs. private** | Different disclosure, investor mix, liquidity, and negotiation dynamics |
| **Broadly syndicated vs. directly originated** | Distribution market dependence vs. relationship-led hold strategy |
| **Investment grade vs. high yield** | Different investor base, pricing expectations, and documentation tolerance |

### Underwriting Relevance

Understanding who provides capital, who controls documentation, and who ultimately owns the risk is essential because:

- pricing alone rarely tells you who has leverage in a transaction;
- structure often reflects distribution needs as much as borrower fundamentals; and
- investor mix affects liquidity, amendment dynamics, and downside behavior in stress.
