# Valuation Cross-Checks

This reference covers the two supporting valuation methods — cost approach and sales comparison — used to cross-check income capitalization in CRE credit underwriting.

---

## Cost Approach

The cost approach is a supporting valuation method. It is most useful when replacement economics matter more than current income evidence.

### Core Formula

```text
Land value
  + replacement or reproduction cost new
  - physical depreciation
  - functional obsolescence
  - external / economic obsolescence
  = indicated value
```

### Best Use Cases

- new or recently delivered assets
- special-purpose properties with limited comparable sales
- insurance or replacement-cost framing
- development and redevelopment feasibility cross-checks

It is usually weaker for older stabilized assets where income and market trading evidence are more informative.

### Why Lenders Care

Replacement cost helps answer:

- would a rational buyer build this asset again at current economics?
- is the subject below, near, or above replacement context?
- does replacement cost create some downside support, or is the asset functionally obsolete?

Replacement context can matter a great deal for newer industrial, multifamily, niche infrastructure, and other capital-intensive property types.

### Depreciation Matters More Than Gross Cost

The critical judgment is often not the raw construction cost, but the deductions:

- **physical depreciation**: wear, age, deferred maintenance
- **functional obsolescence**: poor layout, low utility, outdated systems
- **external obsolescence**: weak market, regulation, neighborhood decline, demand shift

If obsolescence is material, the cost approach can overstate value unless those deductions are treated seriously.

### Use as a Cross-Check, Not a Shortcut

In credit underwriting, the cost approach should usually be presented as:

- a sanity check on income-based value
- a framework for replacement barriers or redevelopment economics
- a way to think about downside support or lack of support

It should not replace income analysis for cash-flowing assets.

### Cost Approach Common Mistakes

- Treating replacement cost as if it guarantees market value
- Underestimating functional or external obsolescence
- Using raw construction cost without considering whether the market would support rebuilding
- Applying cost approach heavily to older stabilized assets where income evidence is stronger

## Sales Comparison Approach

Sales comparison is a market-evidence cross-check. It is strongest when recent comparable transactions exist and weakest when the subject is unique, distressed, highly transitional, or sold into a thin buyer universe.

### Purpose

Use sales comparison to answer:

- whether the income approach is directionally consistent with observed trades
- whether the subject's quality or liquidity warrants a premium or discount
- whether the buyer universe appears deep or narrow

It should rarely be the only valuation method in credit underwriting.

### Choose the Right Unit of Comparison

| Asset Type | Common Comparison Unit |
|---|---|
| **Multifamily** | price per unit, sometimes price per square foot |
| **Office / Retail / Industrial** | price per square foot |
| **Hospitality** | price per key, with operating metrics as support |
| **Specialty assets** | whatever unit best reflects buyer behavior, paired with qualitative adjustment |

Choose the unit the relevant buyer base actually thinks in.

### Select Comps Carefully

Prefer transactions that are similar in:

- property type and functional use
- location and micro-market
- quality, vintage, and physical utility
- tenancy and lease duration
- sale timing and market conditions
- business-plan stage: stabilized, transitional, distressed, or redevelopment

If a transaction is only comparable after many adjustments, it is probably not a strong comp.

### Adjustment Logic

Adjustments should be directional and explainable, not a false exercise in precision.

#### Common adjustment categories

- time / market movement
- location
- quality and age
- occupancy and lease duration
- tenant credit or concentration
- physical utility and scale
- condition, deferred maintenance, or required capital

The more adjustment categories required, the less weight the comp deserves.

### Reconcile with Credit Perspective

A lender should care about more than where the average comparable traded.

#### Ask:

- Would the subject attract the same buyer universe as the comps?
- Are the comps stabilized while the subject still needs execution?
- Are any comps distressed, relationship-driven, or otherwise non-market?
- Would the subject's liquidity disappear faster than the comp set in a stress case?

### Distressed and Transitional Assets

For distressed, vacant, or business-plan-heavy assets:

- separate **as-is** and **stabilized** comparables
- avoid treating sponsor-projected stabilization as if it already exists
- use sales comparison as a range-setting exercise, not a precise answer

If the subject needs major capex or lease-up, the comp analysis must reflect that gap explicitly.

### Sales Comparison Output Template

Show:

1. **Comp table** with the key facts that make each transaction relevant
2. **Adjustment commentary** for the major differences
3. **Weighting rationale** based on similarity and market credibility
4. **Value range** rather than a single manufactured number
5. **Cross-check against income approach**

### Sales Comparison Common Mistakes

- Treating broker-provided comp lists as neutral evidence
- Mixing stabilized and distressed sales without adjusting for business-plan stage
- Using old transactions without discussing market movement
- Over-adjusting weak comps instead of discarding them
- Ignoring buyer depth and liquidity differences between the subject and the comps
