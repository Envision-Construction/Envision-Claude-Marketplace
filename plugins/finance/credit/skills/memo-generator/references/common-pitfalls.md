# Common Credit Memo Pitfalls

This reference catalogs the most frequent and damaging mistakes made in IC credit memos. Each pitfall is described with its root cause, how it manifests in practice, its impact on memo quality and investment decisions, and specific remediation guidance.

---

## 1. Confirmation Bias

### Description
Building the case for a predetermined conclusion rather than conducting an objective analysis and letting the evidence drive the recommendation. This is the single most dangerous pitfall in credit analysis because it directly undermines the purpose of the memo.

### How It Manifests
- Selectively presenting financial data that supports the thesis while omitting contrary data points
- Using management projections as the base case without independent scrutiny
- Dismissing bear-case arguments in a single sentence rather than engaging with them substantively
- Framing risks as unlikely without probability analysis
- Selecting peer comps that make the subject look favorable while excluding comps that do not
- Over-weighting recent positive developments while under-weighting structural weaknesses

### Impact
The IC receives a distorted picture that leads to overconfident positioning, inadequate risk management, and eventual losses when suppressed risks materialize. Persistent confirmation bias erodes the credibility of the analyst and the analytical process.

### Remediation
- Write the risk section before the thesis — force engagement with the bear case early
- Require an explicit "devil's advocate" paragraph or section that argues against the thesis
- Present at least one scenario where the investment loses money and quantify the loss
- Ensure the peer group includes credits that are arguably better alternatives
- Have a colleague review the memo specifically for one-sidedness before submission
- Ask: "If I had the opposite recommendation, what evidence in this memo would support it?"

## 2. Inadequate Downside Analysis

### Description
Presenting only the management case or a mildly stressed scenario without modeling a genuine downside. Credit analysis exists to assess what happens when things go wrong; a memo that only models the base case is fundamentally incomplete.

### How It Manifests
- Single-scenario projections using management guidance
- "Stress" scenarios that assume 5% revenue decline when historical downturns show 15-25% declines
- Downside cases that magically preserve margins and cash flow through cost cuts not yet identified
- No assessment of covenant compliance under stress
- Liquidity analysis that assumes full revolver availability without considering covenant restrictions or borrowing base adjustments
- Stating "the company has adequate liquidity" without modeling the cash burn under stress

### Impact
Position sizing does not reflect actual downside risk. The portfolio is exposed to losses larger than anticipated. IC members cannot properly assess risk-reward because the downside is not quantified.

### Remediation
- Always include at least three scenarios: management/upside, analyst base, and downside/stress
- Calibrate the downside scenario to actual historical experience (prior recession, industry downturn, or company-specific crisis)
- State downside assumptions explicitly: revenue decline, margin compression, working capital consumption, capex reduction
- Show covenant compliance and liquidity under the downside case
- Calculate the maximum loss from the position (spread widening, price decline, principal loss) under stress
- Present downside metrics alongside base case metrics in a side-by-side format

## 3. Generic Risk Sections

### Description
Listing broad risk categories without specificity, quantification, or relevance to the particular credit. Generic risk sections signal that the analyst has not done the work to identify what actually threatens this investment.

### How It Manifests
- "Macroeconomic headwinds could negatively impact performance"
- "Competitive pressures may compress margins"
- "Regulatory changes could affect the business"
- "The company is exposed to interest rate risk"
- Risks copied from previous memos with only the company name changed
- Long lists of theoretical risks with no prioritization or probability assessment
- Mitigants that are equally generic: "Management is monitoring the situation"

### Impact
The IC cannot distinguish between credits with truly manageable risk profiles and those with significant vulnerabilities. Generic risks provide no basis for position sizing, hedging, or monitoring. The risk section becomes a compliance checkbox rather than an analytical tool.

### Remediation
- Every risk must be specific to this credit: name the customer, the regulation, the competitive threat, the covenant
- Quantify the impact: "If Customer X (18% of revenue) does not renew its contract in Q3 2026, EBITDA declines by $45M and leverage increases from 4.2x to 5.1x"
- Assess probability (low/medium/high) and severity (minor/moderate/severe) for each risk
- Limit the list to three to six genuinely material risks rather than enumerating every conceivable concern
- Pair each risk with a specific, credible mitigant — not a general statement of awareness
- Prioritize by probability-weighted impact, with the most dangerous risk first

## 4. Missing Relative Value Context

### Description
Analyzing a credit in isolation without positioning it against comparable alternatives. Fundamental analysis answers "is this a good credit?" but relative value answers "is this a good investment?" — the second question is what the IC needs answered.

### How It Manifests
- No peer comparison section at all
- Peer comparison limited to a spread table without analytical commentary
- Stating the credit is "attractive" without quantifying the basis (in bps) relative to alternatives
- Comps selected to make the subject look favorable rather than to provide honest context
- No explanation of why a valuation differential exists
- No catalyst identified for spread convergence

### Impact
The IC cannot assess opportunity cost. Capital may be allocated to credits that are fundamentally sound but fairly valued when better risk-adjusted alternatives exist. Without relative value context, the memo reads as an equity research report rather than a fixed income analysis.

### Remediation
- Include a comp table with four to eight peers showing: rating, spread, leverage, coverage, and at least one quality metric
- State the quantified basis: "Trading 65bps wide of the BB industrials median"
- Explain the reason for the differential: rating gap, size discount, event-specific widening, liquidity premium
- Assess whether the differential is justified or represents mispricing
- Identify what will cause convergence and the expected timeline
- Include at least one cross-market comparison (bonds vs. loans, or vs. CDS) where relevant

## 5. Stale Market Data

### Description
Using spread, price, or yield data that does not reflect current market conditions. In credit markets, spreads can move 50-100bps in a week during volatile periods. Stale data leads to stale recommendations.

### How It Manifests
- Market data from weeks or months before the memo date without disclosure
- New issue pricing used as "current" levels long after the deal has traded away from reoffer
- Peer spreads pulled from different dates, making comparisons invalid
- No date stamps on market references
- Relative value conclusions based on pre-event spread levels
- Using index data or generic curves rather than actual traded levels

### Impact
Recommendations based on stale data may already be priced in or no longer valid. The IC makes decisions on outdated information, leading to poor execution or missed opportunities.

### Remediation
- Date-stamp every market data reference in the memo
- Use spread and price data no more than one to two business days old for the subject credit
- Pull all peer comps as of the same date
- Note any material spread moves since the data was pulled
- If the memo was drafted over multiple days, update market data on the day of submission
- Reference actual traded levels or executable quotes rather than model-derived estimates where possible

## 6. Recommendation Disconnected from Analysis

### Description
The recommendation does not logically follow from the analysis presented in the memo. This occurs when the analyst reaches a conclusion before writing the memo and fails to reconcile contradictory evidence.

### How It Manifests
- Bullish analysis followed by a "hold" recommendation without explaining the disconnect
- Identifying significant risks in the risk section but recommending a large position
- Projecting deteriorating credit metrics but maintaining a buy recommendation based on spread
- Relative value section showing the credit is rich to peers but recommending a buy based on fundamentals
- High-conviction recommendation supported by tentative or hedged analysis
- Downside scenario showing material loss potential with no reflection in position sizing

### Impact
The IC loses trust in the analytical process. Inconsistencies create confusion about the actual view and lead to allocation decisions that do not match the underlying risk assessment.

### Remediation
- Write the recommendation last, after completing all analytical sections
- Before finalizing, re-read the executive summary, risk section, and relative value section — ask whether the recommendation is consistent with each
- If analysis is mixed, the conviction level should reflect that (medium or low, not high)
- If risks are material, position sizing should be conservative
- Explicitly address contradictions: "Despite elevated leverage of 5.5x, we recommend a buy because the deleveraging path is credible and the 80bps spread pickup compensates for the additional risk"
- Have a colleague check for consistency between analysis and recommendation

## 7. Over-Reliance on Rating Agency Views

### Description
Treating credit ratings as the primary analytical input rather than conducting independent analysis. Rating agencies provide useful frameworks but have known limitations: they are backward-looking, slow to act, and subject to conflicts.

### How It Manifests
- Using the rating as the lead indicator of credit quality without independent assessment
- Anchoring leverage and coverage expectations to rating agency thresholds rather than analytical judgment
- Citing rating agency reports as primary sources for business or financial analysis
- Treating a rating upgrade or downgrade as a thesis in itself without independent validation
- Aligning relative value expectations to rating category averages without quality-adjusting

### Impact
The portfolio effectively outsources credit judgment to rating agencies, negating the value of in-house analysis. The portfolio misses opportunities where ratings lag fundamentals (both upgrades and downgrades) and faces concentrated losses when rating agency assumptions prove wrong.

### Remediation
- State the rating and rating agency outlook, then immediately present the independent analyst view — including where and why it differs
- Use rating agency methodologies as one input, not the sole framework
- When a rating action is part of the thesis, validate the underlying fundamental assumptions independently
- Note where the analyst view diverges from the rating: "Moody's Ba2 rating is based on 4.5x leverage; our adjusted leverage is 5.1x after removing $30M of questionable add-backs"
- Assess the probability and timing of rating migration independently

## 8. Missing ESG and Sustainability Considerations

### Description
Omitting or trivializing ESG factors that are material to credit quality. ESG is not a separate analytical silo — environmental, social, and governance risks directly affect cash flows, access to capital, and regulatory exposure.

### How It Manifests
- No ESG section at all
- A two-line ESG section that says "no material ESG concerns identified" without analysis
- Treating ESG as a compliance requirement rather than a credit-relevant analytical dimension
- Ignoring sustainability-linked loan or bond features and their financial implications
- Failing to assess governance quality (board independence, management compensation, capital allocation discipline) as a credit factor
- Overlooking transition risks for carbon-intensive industries

### Impact
Material risks are not identified until they crystallize as financial events (regulatory fines, litigation, stranded assets, reputational damage). The portfolio may face unexpected losses from ESG-related developments. Increasingly, institutional investors require ESG integration, and its absence undermines credibility.

### Remediation
- Assess materiality of E, S, and G factors specifically for this issuer and industry
- For environmental: quantify carbon exposure, regulatory compliance costs, transition risk timeline
- For social: evaluate labor practices, supply chain risk, product safety record, community relations
- For governance: assess board independence, executive compensation alignment with credit outcomes, track record of management integrity and capital allocation
- If sustainability-linked features exist, analyze the KPIs, likelihood of achieving targets, and financial impact of step-ups or step-downs
- Link ESG factors to specific credit risks or strengths — do not treat the section as standalone

## 9. Insufficient Covenant Analysis for Leveraged Credits

### Description
Describing covenants without analyzing their effectiveness, headroom, or potential for erosion. For leveraged credits, covenants are the primary structural protection — a memo that does not analyze them thoroughly is incomplete.

### How It Manifests
- Listing covenant types (leverage, coverage) without calculating current headroom
- Showing current compliance without projecting forward under base and stress cases
- Ignoring EBITDA add-back provisions that inflate compliance EBITDA vs. actual EBITDA
- Not analyzing key permitted baskets (incremental debt, restricted payments, investment baskets)
- Treating "covenant-lite" as a description rather than a risk to be analyzed
- Ignoring equity cure provisions and their impact on lender protections
- Not assessing J. Crew, Chewy, or similar liability management transaction risk in weak documentation

### Impact
The analyst overestimates structural protection. Covenant headroom is smaller than reported when add-backs are adjusted. Permitted baskets may allow value leakage before maintenance covenants are tested. The portfolio is exposed to structural subordination and asset stripping.

### Remediation
- Calculate covenant headroom using both reported EBITDA (with add-backs) and adjusted EBITDA (without questionable add-backs)
- Project quarterly covenant compliance under base and stress cases for the next eight quarters
- Quantify the largest permitted baskets: incremental debt capacity, restricted payment capacity, investment basket capacity
- Assess whether the covenant package is tight, moderate, or loose relative to current leverage
- For covenant-lite deals: identify what structural protections remain and assess their adequacy
- Compare the documentation to the sponsor's precedent deals to identify unusual provisions
- Specifically flag liability management transaction risk if documentation permits unrestricted subsidiary designations, asset transfers, or layered priming debt

## 10. No Clear Catalysts or Monitoring Triggers

### Description
Recommending a credit without identifying the specific events that will drive value realization or the metrics that should trigger a reassessment. A recommendation without catalysts is a hope; a recommendation without monitoring triggers is a forgotten position.

### How It Manifests
- "We expect spreads to tighten over time" without identifying why or when
- Catalysts described as trends rather than events ("improving fundamentals" vs. "Q3 earnings expected to confirm 200bps margin expansion from plant closure")
- No stop-loss or thesis violation trigger defined
- No monitoring framework: the memo is written, the position is established, and the credit is not revisited until a problem arises
- Catalysts with no timeline attached
- Mixing catalysts (events that drive the thesis) with hopes (things that would be nice)

### Impact
Positions drift without accountability. Catalysts that were expected to occur in six months remain "expected" two years later. Without monitoring triggers, deteriorating credits are not identified until losses are significant. The IC cannot assess whether a position is working as expected.

### Remediation
- Every catalyst must be a specific, observable event with an expected timeline: "Rating upgrade expected following Q2 2026 earnings when trailing leverage drops below 4.0x Moody's upgrade threshold"
- Distinguish between catalysts (high probability events driving value) and optionality (lower probability upside events)
- Define stop-loss triggers in measurable terms: spread level, price, leverage threshold, or rating action
- Define thesis violation indicators: specific outcomes that mean the investment thesis has failed
- Establish a monitoring framework: key metrics to track, data sources, frequency of review
- Set a next-review date explicitly in the memo
- At each review, assess whether catalysts have occurred on schedule and whether monitoring triggers have been hit
